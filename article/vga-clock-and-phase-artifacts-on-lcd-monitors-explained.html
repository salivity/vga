VGA Clock and Phase Artifacts on LCD Monitors Explained

Connecting an LCD monitor to a computer using an analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) cable often results in distracting visual noise, such as pixel shimmering, fuzzy text, and vertical banding. This phenomenon occurs because of the fundamental incompatibility between continuous analog signals and discrete digital pixel grids, requiring the monitor's internal Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) to precisely reconstruct the incoming image. When the monitor’s internal sampling frequency or timing deviates even slightly from the graphics card’s output, clock and phase artifacts appear on the screen.

The Core Problem: Analog-to-Digital Conversion

Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitors were purely analog devices that swept an electron beam continuously across a phosphor screen, rendering the exact timing of individual pixel transitions relatively forgiving. Liquid Crystal Displays (LCDs), by contrast, consist of a rigid matrix of physical pixels with fixed coordinates.

When a graphics card outputs a VGA signal, it converts digital frame data into an analog voltage waveform using a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC). The LCD monitor must then perform the reverse process using an ADC to sample this continuous voltage stream and map it back into individual, discrete pixel values. If this sampling is not perfectly synchronized in both rate (clock) and timing (phase), artifacts become visible.

Clock Mismatch and Vertical Banding

The "clock" parameter determines the total number of samples the monitor takes across a single horizontal scanline. This includes not only the visible horizontal resolution (such as 1920 pixels) but also the non-visible blanking intervals dictated by the video timing standard.

If the monitor's internal clock does not match the exact pixel clock generated by the graphics card, the sampling rate drifts:

  • Under-sampling: The monitor samples too few times per line, causing it to periodically miss pixels.
  • Over-sampling: The monitor samples too frequently, duplicating pixels.

This frequency mismatch produces distinct, wide vertical bands or shimmering stripes across the display. In these bands, details appear stretched or compressed, and any high-contrast pattern—such as alternating single-pixel black and white lines—will display noticeable interference patterns resembling a moiré effect.

Phase Misalignment and Shimmering Noise

Even when the monitor's clock frequency accurately matches the graphics card's pixel clock, the samples must be taken at the precise moment each pixel’s voltage is stable. This timing offset is the "phase."

An analog video signal does not instantly jump from one color or brightness value to another; the voltage requires a brief fraction of a nanosecond to rise or fall between adjacent pixels. Consequently, each pixel window consists of:

  1. An unstable transition period (rise/fall time).
  2. A stable plateau representing the true color/brightness value.

Phase adjustments shift the exact sampling instant forward or backward in time within the pixel window. If the phase is properly aligned, the ADC samples the signal dead-center on the stable plateau. If the phase is misaligned, the ADC samples the signal during the voltage transition. Because this transition region is volatile and subject to electrical noise, the ADC fluctuates between adjacent brightness values from frame to frame. This manifests as fine, high-frequency "fuzziness" or shimmering, most visible around text edges, high-contrast borders, and dithered backgrounds.

Why Digital Connections Eliminate the Issue

Modern digital interfaces, such as DVI, HDMI, and DisplayPort, transmit image data as discrete numerical values using high-speed digital protocols. Because the monitor receives exact binary numbers for each sub-pixel rather than a continuous voltage that must be re-digitized, there is no analog sampling stage. As a result, clock drift, phase misalignment, and the resulting shimmering artifacts are completely eliminated on digital connections.
