How CRT Electron Guns Sweep in VGA Displays

A Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitor running a Video Graphics Array (VGA) interface draws images by sweeping focused beams of electrons horizontally and vertically across a phosphor-coated glass faceplate. Controlled by electromagnetic coils known as the deflection yoke, the electron beams scan line-by-line from left to right and top to bottom in a continuous raster pattern. This movement is strictly governed by analog VGA horizontal and vertical synchronization pulses, translating electrical signals from a graphics card into real-time physical beam trajectories that generate a visible progressive display.

The Role of the Electron Gun and Deflection Yoke

The base of a color CRT contains three electron guns (one for each primary color: red, green, and blue). Heated cathodes emit electrons through thermionic emission, which are then focused into tight beams and accelerated by high positive voltage toward the screen's anode.

Before reaching the screen, the beams pass through the deflection yoke positioned on the neck of the CRT. The yoke contains two independent sets of copper electromagnetic coils: one for horizontal deflection and one for vertical deflection. Passing an electric current through these coils generates magnetic fields that bend the path of the negatively charged electrons via the Lorentz force.

Horizontal Sweeping

The horizontal sweep draws individual scan lines across the screen.

  1. Active Trace (Left to Right): A linearly ramping "sawtooth" electric current passes through the horizontal deflection coils. As current increases, the magnetic field strength changes smoothly, pushing the electron beams horizontally from the far-left edge of the visible screen to the far-right edge. As the beams travel, varying analog voltages on the VGA red, green, and blue pins modulate the beam intensity, illuminating phosphors at different brightness levels to draw pixels.
  2. Horizontal Blanking and Retrace (Right to Left): Once the beam reaches the right edge, the graphics adapter sends a Horizontal Sync (HSync) pulse. The deflection circuit rapidly reverses the current, snapping the beam back to the left edge of the screen—a process called horizontal retrace or flyback. During this brief transit, the electron gun is switched off (horizontal blanking) so no visible line is drawn backward across the display.

In standard VGA mode (640x480 resolution at 60 Hz), the horizontal sweep operates at a frequency of approximately 31.468 kHz. This means the beam executes over 31,400 horizontal sweeps every second, completing one full scan line in roughly 31.77 microseconds.

Vertical Sweeping

While the horizontal coils sweep the beam rapidly across the screen, the vertical deflection coils slowly pull the beam downward.

  1. Vertical Downward Trace: A secondary, much slower sawtooth current feeds the vertical deflection coils. This field steadily shifts the horizontal sweep lines downward, one after another, creating a tight stack of parallel lines that form a full frame (raster).
  2. Vertical Blanking and Retrace: After the beam finishes drawing the bottom-most line (line 480 in standard VGA), the graphics card sends a Vertical Sync (VSync) pulse. The vertical circuit quickly collapses and reverses its magnetic field, returning the beam to the top-left corner of the display. Just like horizontal retrace, the beam is blanked during this vertical retrace period to prevent visible diagonal lines across the image.

In a standard 60 Hz VGA setup, the vertical sweep repeats 60 times per second, drawing 525 total lines per frame (480 visible active lines plus 45 blanked lines reserved for timing stabilization and vertical retrace).

VGA Synchronization

A standard VGA connection uses separate physical lines for Horizontal Sync (pin 13) and Vertical Sync (pin 14). Unlike older composite video systems where synchronization signals were combined with video information, standard VGA feeds discrete TTL timing pulses to the monitor's internal deflection circuitry.

These sync pulses act as precise triggers for the flyback circuits. The HSync pulse signals the horizontal oscillator to initiate flyback to the left, while the VSync pulse triggers the vertical oscillator to reset to the top. By locking the sawtooth magnetic deflection to these incoming pulses, the monitor ensures that the beam lands on the correct phosphor dot at the exact microsecond the graphics card transmits the corresponding RGB color voltages.
