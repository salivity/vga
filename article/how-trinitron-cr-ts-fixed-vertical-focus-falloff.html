How Trinitron CRTs Fixed Vertical Focus Falloff

Traditional curved shadow mask Video Graphics Array (VGA) cathode ray tubes (CRTs) frequently suffered from vertical focus falloff, where text and imagery lost sharpness toward the top and bottom edges of the screen. Sony’s Trinitron aperture grille architecture eliminated this flaw by combining a vertically flat cylindrical faceplate, an unbroken vertical wire grille, and a single large-aperture electron gun lens. Together, these mechanical and optical innovations removed the compound geometric errors and optical aberrations that plagued conventional spherically curved monitors.

Conventional VGA monitors utilized a shadow mask system with a spherical glass surface curved across both the horizontal and vertical axes. As the electron beams swept vertically away from the center toward the top or bottom edges, the throw distance from the electron gun to the phosphor screen changed non-linearly. Because standard electron guns used three separate, smaller focusing lenses—one for each color channel—they lacked the optical capacity to correct for severe spherical aberration. The resulting electron beam elongated and dispersed, creating spot-shape distortion and noticeable softening along the vertical margins.

The Trinitron solved the geometric aspect of this issue by adopting a cylindrical screen profile rather than a spherical one. The display glass was curved horizontally to maintain structural rigidity against vacuum pressure, but it was completely flat along the vertical plane. By maintaining a uniform vertical profile, the rate of change in focal distance during the vertical sweep was drastically simplified. This predictable geometry eliminated the complex, multi-axis focal shifts that standard dynamic focus circuits struggled to compensate for on spherical displays.

In place of a perforated metal sheet with individual circular holes, the aperture grille used a series of tightly tensioned, continuous vertical wires. Because there were no horizontal grid lines or crossbars dividing the phosphor stripes, the electron beam encountered no vertical obstruction or inter-hole pitch boundaries. The continuous vertical stripes allowed the beam spot to land uniformly across the display height without being clipped or creating the vertical moiré-induced beam spreading typical of shadow masks.

The optical core of the Trinitron system was its single-gun, three-beam design featuring a large-aperture focus lens. Instead of packing three small, compromised lenses inside the tube's neck, Sony utilized a single lens with a significantly larger diameter through which all three electron beams passed. In optical physics, a larger lens diameter dramatically reduces spherical aberration. This allowed the Trinitron to project a much tighter, more circular, and symmetrically concentrated electron beam.

Because the large lens minimized inherent beam distortion and the cylindrical faceplate neutralized compound vertical throw variations, the CRT’s dynamic vertical focus circuitry could operate with extreme precision. The beam remained crisply focused from the exact center of the monitor directly to the extreme upper and lower edges, maintaining uniform sharpness across entire high-resolution VGA desktop environments.
