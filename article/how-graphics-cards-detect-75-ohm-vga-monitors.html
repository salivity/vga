How Graphics Cards Detect 75-Ohm VGA Monitors

Graphics processing units (GPUs) detect connected Video Graphics Array (VGA) monitors by measuring the electrical impedance on the analog color lines. Standard VGA displays incorporate 75-ohm termination resistors to ground on each of the Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) video channels to prevent signal reflection. By pulsing a small test current through these lines and measuring the resulting voltage drop using integrated analog comparators, the graphics card’s Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) can reliably confirm whether a monitor is physically plugged in.

The Role of 75-Ohm Termination

The VGA standard relies on analog video transmission across coaxial lines with a characteristic impedance of 75 ohms. To maintain signal integrity and eliminate signal reflections that cause visual artifacts like ghosting, both the transmitter (the graphics card) and the receiver (the monitor) must match this impedance.

Inside the monitor, each RGB input pin is connected to a 75-ohm resistor tied directly to ground. When no monitor is attached, the VGA connector pins sit as an open circuit (infinite impedance). When a monitor is connected, the line impedance drops to 75 ohms.

DAC Current Injection and Voltage Sensing

Most graphics cards utilize current-output video DACs (historically known as RAMDACs). Rather than directly outputting a static voltage, these DACs drive a controlled current across the video line.

To test for monitor presence, the GPU initiates a detection routine:

  1. Current Injection: The DAC drives a known, calibrated current into the RGB output channels.
  2. Voltage Development: According to Ohm's Law (\(V = I \times R\)), this current produces a voltage across whatever load is attached to the pin.
    • Terminated Line (Monitor Connected): The injected current flows through the monitor's 75-ohm resistor to ground, pulling the line to a nominal voltage level (typically around 0.7 volts for full white scale, or a lower test threshold depending on the DAC implementation).
    • Unterminated Line (No Monitor): Because the pin is an open circuit, the current has no return path to ground through a 75-ohm load. This causes the line voltage to float upward toward the DAC’s internal power supply rail (often 3.3V or 5V in older hardware).

Analog Comparators and Detection Logic

Inside the display engine of the GPU, each analog video pin is wired to an internal analog comparator circuit.

The comparator compares the real-time line voltage against a predetermined reference threshold voltage (commonly set slightly higher than the standard 0.7V peak video signal, such as 1.0V to 1.2V).

  • If the sensed voltage remains below the threshold, the circuit registers that the 75-ohm load is present, successfully sinking the current. The GPU sets an internal status bit indicating that a display device is detected on that channel.
  • If the sensed voltage rises above the threshold, the circuit identifies an open circuit. The GPU determines that no display is connected.

Detection Timing and Display Data Channel (DDC)

Load detection is primarily used during boot-up, driver initialization, or hot-plug polling routines. Because driving DC current onto the RGB lines can cause brief flashes on an active display, modern drivers execute this check during the vertical blanking interval or when no active digital communication exists.

While modern setups also query the monitor’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) via the I2C-based Display Data Channel (DDC), 75-ohm load sensing remains the foundational low-level hardware method used by GPUs to verify physical analog connectivity, especially for legacy monitors that lack DDC support.
