Why Moiré Patterns Occur on High-Res VGA CRTs

Moiré patterns on a high-resolution Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitor driven by a Video Graphics Array (VGA) interface are optical interference artifacts caused by a spatial mismatch between the analog raster scanlines and the physical phosphor mask inside the display. When the spacing of the electronically drawn pixels or scanlines closely approaches the mechanical pitch of the monitor's shadow mask or aperture grille, the two periodic grids beat against each other. This article explains the mechanics of CRT image formation, the physics of spatial aliasing, why higher VGA resolutions trigger the effect, and how these visual artifacts are typically mitigated.

The Two Overlapping Grids

To understand CRT moiré, one must look at the two distinct grid systems that form the final image:

  1. The Physical Display Mask: Behind the glass faceplate of a color CRT lies a metal screen—typically a shadow mask, slot mask, or aperture grille. This mask features microscopic perforations or vertical wires with a fixed physical distance between them, known as the dot pitch or stripe pitch (commonly 0.20 mm to 0.28 mm). This structure ensures that electron beams precisely strike their intended red, green, or blue phosphors.
  2. The Electron Beam Raster: A VGA controller outputs an analog video signal that directs one or more electron guns to sweep horizontally across the screen, line by line, from top to bottom. The number of active scanlines and the pixel clock frequency define an electronically generated grid of light.

The Physics of Moiré Interference

Moiré is a classic example of spatial aliasing or mechanical heterodyning. When two regular, repetitive geometric patterns are overlaid, they produce a new, lower-frequency secondary pattern if their spatial frequencies are close to one another or to harmonic multiples.

In mathematical terms, if the frequency of the raster image lines is \(f_1\) and the frequency of the physical aperture mask is \(f_2\), the superposition of the two produces sum (\(f_1 + f_2\)) and difference (\(|f_1 - f_2|\)) frequencies. While the sum frequency is too fine for the human eye to perceive, the difference frequency often falls within the visible spatial spectrum, manifesting as wavy, dark-and-light bands or ripple patterns across the screen.

Why High-Resolution VGA Drives Moiré

Moiré patterns rarely appear at standard legacy VGA resolutions (such as 640×480) because the spacing between scanlines is significantly wider than the display's dot pitch. As display resolutions scale up over an analog VGA connection to 1024×768, 1280×1024, or 1600×1200:

  • Vertical Moiré: Higher resolutions compress more horizontal scanlines into the same vertical physical height. When the distance between consecutive scanlines approaches the vertical pitch of the shadow mask holes, optical interference occurs along the vertical axis.
  • Horizontal Moiré: High-resolution signals demand higher pixel clock rates. Alternating pixel patterns (such as single-pixel vertical lines or fine dithering) cause the beam intensity to pulse at a frequency that closely aligns with the horizontal pitch of the aperture grille or mask slots, producing vertical or diagonal ripple bands.
  • Analog Bandwidth and Sharpness: High-end CRT monitors designed for high-resolution VGA had tightly focused electron beams. An extremely sharp, narrow beam profile increases contrast between adjacent scanlines, heightening the intensity of the raster grid and making moiré interference far more pronounced than it would be on a softer, slightly defocused monitor.

Mitigation Techniques

Because the physical mask cannot be altered, eliminating moiré requires disrupting the alignment between the two frequencies:

  • Changing Display Resolutions or Refresh Rates: Switching to a slightly different vertical line count or changing the vertical refresh rate alters the beam velocity and raster spacing, shifting the beat frequency outside the visible range.
  • Electronic Moiré Cancellation: Advanced CRT monitors include internal "Moiré Cancellation" circuits. These circuits subtly alter the electron beam by applying a tiny high-frequency dither or slight horizontal/vertical beam oscillation, effectively softening the sharp edges of individual scanlines so they blend more uniformly across the mask.
  • Beam Defocusing: Slightly reducing the display's focus voltage broadens the electron beam spot size. While this slightly reduces overall text sharpness, it prevents the raster lines from forming the sharp, discrete boundaries that generate interference patterns.
