Why VGA Monitors Need 75-Ohm Termination Resistors

A standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) monitor requires 75-ohm termination resistors on its red, green, and blue (RGB) color lines to prevent signal reflections, maintain signal integrity, and establish standard voltage levels for accurate color display. Because VGA transmits high-frequency analog video signals over coaxial wiring, matching the monitor's input impedance to the cable's 75-ohm characteristic impedance ensures that all transmitted energy is properly absorbed, preventing visual artifacts such as ghosting, ringing, and color distortion.

Transmission Line Behavior and Impedance Matching

VGA color signals operate at high frequencies, often exceeding 100 MHz at higher resolutions and refresh rates. At these frequencies, the wavelengths of the electrical signals become comparable to or shorter than the length of the VGA cable. Consequently, the cable can no longer be treated as a simple wire; it functions as a transmission line.

Standard VGA cables use miniature coaxial cables for each of the primary color lines, manufactured to have a characteristic impedance (\(Z_0\)) of precisely 75 ohms. When an electrical wave travels down a transmission line and encounters a boundary, the proportion of the wave reflected back toward the source depends on the difference between the line's impedance and the load's impedance. By placing a 75-ohm resistor to ground at the monitor's input stage, the load impedance matches the transmission line's characteristic impedance. This matching allows the monitor to absorb the maximum amount of signal energy without reflecting it back into the cable.

Preventing Visual Ghosting and Ringing

If the color lines were left unterminated (presenting high impedance) or improperly terminated, a significant portion of the incoming video signal would reflect off the monitor's input and travel back toward the graphics card. Once the reflected signal reaches the graphics card, it can reflect again toward the monitor, arriving with a slight time delay.

On the display, this delayed signal appears as a faint, duplicate image shifted horizontally to the right of the intended image—an artifact known as ghosting. Improper termination can also cause high-frequency oscillations known as ringing, visible as halo effects or repeating dark and light bands along high-contrast edges (such as black text on a white background). The 75-ohm termination completely dampens these reflected waves, ensuring crisp edges and sharp transitions.

Voltage Standardization and Dynamic Range

The 75-ohm load is an essential part of the circuit that determines the signal's voltage levels. The video digital-to-analog converter (RAMDAC) on a graphics card is typically designed as a current source, or as a voltage source with its own internal 75-ohm source impedance.

According to the VESA (Video Electronics Standards Association) standard, VGA color signals require a dynamic range of 0 to 0.7 volts peak-to-peak:

  • 0.0 V represents absolute black (no illumination).
  • 0.7 V represents maximum color intensity.

When the graphics card outputs current down the line, that current passes through the monitor's 75-ohm termination resistor to ground. By Ohm's Law (\(V = I \times R\)), this develops the expected 0.7V maximum signal level across the load. If the 75-ohm termination resistor were missing, the signal voltage would double to roughly 1.4V, causing the display to be severely over-saturated, washed out, or potentially damaging the input circuitry of the monitor.
