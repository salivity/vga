800x600 Resolution Standard Explained

The 800x600 display resolution is formally designated as Super Video Graphics Array, commonly abbreviated as SVGA. This article covers the origins of the SVGA standard, how it improved upon the baseline Video Graphics Array (VGA) specification, its technical properties, and its enduring role in computer display history.

Standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) was introduced by IBM in 1987, establishing a standard resolution of 640x480 pixels with up to 16 colors, or 320x200 pixels with 256 colors. As monitor hardware and graphics chipsets rapidly advanced in the late 1980s, manufacturers began developing hardware capable of surpassing VGA capabilities. The resulting specification was termed Super VGA (SVGA), which established 800x600 pixels as the next universal display tier.

To eliminate compatibility issues across different hardware vendors offering proprietary extensions, the Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) was founded in 1989. VESA standardized SVGA, creating uniform software interfaces that allowed software developers to target 800x600 resolution reliably across various graphics cards.

From a technical perspective, an 800x600 display retains the traditional 4:3 aspect ratio used by standard VGA and classic CRT monitors. It contains a total of 480,000 distinct pixels per frame—a 56% increase in screen workspace compared to standard 640x480 VGA (307,200 pixels). SVGA also supported higher color depths, allowing systems to display thousands (16-bit High Color) or millions (24-bit True Color) of colors simultaneously at this resolution, depending on the available video RAM.

During the 1990s, SVGA became the mainstream baseline standard for personal computer operating systems and PC gaming before eventually being superseded by Extended Graphics Array (XGA at 1024x768) and modern widescreen high-definition formats. Today, the 800x600 SVGA standard remains relevant as a legacy fallback resolution for basic input/output systems (BIOS/UEFI), system recovery environments, virtualization software, and embedded industrial displays.
