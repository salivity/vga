How VGA Sync Polarity Sets Vertical Resolution

This article explains how the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard communicates vertical resolution to analog monitors using the electrical polarity of synchronization signals. Before digital display communication standards like DDC and EDID were introduced, early CRT monitors relied on simple hardware signaling to adapt their internal deflection circuits. By toggling the horizontal and vertical synchronization pulses between positive (active-high) and negative (active-low) states, a graphics card encodes the targeted vertical line count directly into the timing signals.

The Role of Synchronization Signals

An analog VGA connection transmits image data as separate red, green, and blue (RGB) color voltages alongside two dedicated timing lines: Horizontal Sync (H-Sync) and Vertical Sync (V-Sync). H-Sync tells the monitor when to move the electron beam to the next scanline, while V-Sync tells the monitor to return to the top of the screen for the next frame.

Because early VGA monitors had to support multiple display modes with varying vertical line counts (primarily 350, 400, and 480 lines) at standard refresh rates, the display needed a mechanism to detect the incoming format and adjust its vertical size (height) accordingly. Without this adjustment, modes with fewer vertical lines would appear vertically compressed or stretched out of proportion.

The 2-Bit Polarity Encoding Scheme

VGA solved this format-detection problem by assigning meaning to the resting state and pulse direction of the H-Sync and V-Sync signals. A pulse is "negative" (active-low) if the signal line normally sits at a high voltage (approximately +5V) and drops to 0V during the sync event. A pulse is "positive" (active-high) if the line normally sits at 0V and pulses up to +5V during the sync event.

By pairing the polarities of H-Sync and V-Sync, the VGA controller forms a simple 2-bit state machine that defines the active vertical lines:

  • Negative H-Sync, Positive V-Sync: Indicates 350 lines. This mode provided backward compatibility with the Enhanced Graphics Adapter (EGA) standard, typically operating at a 70 Hz refresh rate (e.g., 720×350).
  • Positive H-Sync, Negative V-Sync: Indicates 400 lines. This mode was commonly used for standard DOS text displays (e.g., 720×400 text mode) operating at a 70 Hz refresh rate.
  • Negative H-Sync, Negative V-Sync: Indicates 480 lines. This mode represents standard VGA graphics (e.g., 640×480) operating at a 60 Hz refresh rate.
  • Positive H-Sync, Positive V-Sync: Originally reserved by IBM for future display modes, this combination was later adopted by VESA standards to signal higher-resolution modes, such as 800×600 (SVGA) and 1024×768 (XGA).

Hardware Detection and Deflection Control

Inside the CRT monitor, hardware logic gates or microcontrollers continuously detect the polarity of the sync pulses. For pure analog chassis designs, simple RC (resistor-capacitor) integrators or diode networks differentiate the pulses to generate control voltages.

When a mode switch occurs:

  1. The monitor's sync-processing circuit identifies the polarity combination of the H-Sync and V-Sync lines.
  2. The detected combination switches specific resistor networks or adjusts the gain inside the vertical deflection amplifier.
  3. Modifying the vertical deflection current changes the physical angle of the electron beam's sweep, ensuring that 350, 400, or 480 active scanlines occupy the full physical height of the display tube with the correct 4:3 aspect ratio.

This hardware-level signaling allowed displays to automatically configure vertical scanning parameters instantaneously without requiring bidirectional digital communication channels. Modern standards eventually superseded this mechanism with the I²C-based Display Data Channel (DDC), but sync polarity encoding remains a foundational feature of legacy VGA timing specifications.
