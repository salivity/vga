VGA Cable Impedance Mismatch and Standing Waves

Electrical impedance mismatching along a Video Graphics Array (VGA) cable causes high-frequency analog video signals to reflect off interfaces where impedance changes occur, such as poor cable terminations or degraded connectors. These reflected waves bounce back and forth, interacting with the original incoming signals to generate standing waves, which visibly degrade the display output by introducing artifacts such as ghosting, ringing, and color smearing.

A standard VGA transmission system is designed around a continuous characteristic impedance of 75 ohms (\(\Omega\)). This standard applies uniformly across the signal source (the graphics processing unit's digital-to-analog converter), the internal coaxial conductors of the VGA cable, and the termination load inside the monitor. When all components share this exact 75-ohm impedance, the electrical load absorbs the entirety of the incident signal energy, allowing the analog color information (Red, Green, and Blue channels) to be accurately translated into screen pixels without distortion.

An impedance mismatch occurs when the characteristic impedance of the cable (\(Z_0\)) differs from the termination impedance of the load (\(Z_L\)) or the source. This discrepancy commonly arises from low-quality cables with irregular wire geometries, damaged shielding, sharp bends, or uncalibrated input terminations on the display. According to transmission line theory, the voltage reflection coefficient (\(\Gamma\)) governs the behavior at any boundary where impedance changes:

\[\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}\]

If \(Z_L\) equals \(Z_0\), the reflection coefficient is zero, meaning no energy is reflected. However, if a mismatch exists—for instance, if \(Z_L\) is higher or lower than 75 ohms—\(\Gamma\) becomes a non-zero value. Instead of being completely absorbed by the monitor, a fraction of the electrical signal's energy reflects off the load interface and travels backward toward the source.

As the reflected wave propagates backward, it encounters the source end of the cable. If the source impedance is not perfectly matched to absorb incoming reverse currents, the wave reflects again, rebounding back toward the display. The simultaneous presence of forward-traveling (incident) waves and reverse-traveling (reflected) waves produces constructive and destructive interference along the cable. This interference pattern forms a standing wave, characterized by stationary points of minimum voltage (nodes) and maximum voltage (antinodes) at fixed intervals dictated by the signal's frequency and propagation velocity.

Because VGA signals carry high-frequency analog data—often exceeding 100 MHz for higher resolutions and refresh rates—these reflections arrive at the display shifted in time relative to the original signal. The delayed voltage spikes superimpose onto subsequent pixel data, directly altering the intended voltage levels of the RGB channels. On screen, this manifests as double images, faint duplicate outlines to the right of high-contrast edges (ghosting), and oscillating edge halos (ringing).
