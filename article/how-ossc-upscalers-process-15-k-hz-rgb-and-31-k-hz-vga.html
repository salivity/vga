How OSSC Upscalers Process 15 kHz RGB and 31 kHz VGA

Modern retro upscalers like the Open Source Scan Converter (OSSC) bridge the gap between legacy gaming systems and contemporary digital displays by handling both standard-definition 15 kHz signals and higher-resolution 31 kHz signals. Rather than using frame buffers that introduce noticeable display lag, these devices utilize high-speed analog front-ends and field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) to dynamically sample, process, and line-multiply analog video in real time. This article details the hardware pipeline and synchronization methods that enable devices like the OSSC to seamlessly switch between and upscale these disparate horizontal scan rates.

Understanding 15 kHz RGB vs. 31 kHz VGA

Legacy video consoles output video across differing scan rates based on their generation and target display technology:

  • 15 kHz (Standard Definition): Used by consoles from the Atari 2600 up to the Sony PlayStation and Nintendo 64. The horizontal scan rate sits near 15.7 kHz, drawing either alternating fields for 480i resolution or non-interlaced progressive frames for 240p resolution.
  • 31 kHz (Enhanced Definition/VGA): Popularized by the Sega Dreamcast, arcade hardware, and vintage PCs. The horizontal scan rate sits near 31.5 kHz, drawing 480 progressive lines (480p) at 60 Hz by scanning twice as fast as a standard-definition television.

Signal Digitization via the Analog Front-End

The conversion process begins at the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). Devices like the OSSC use high-performance video digitizers, such as the Texas Instruments TVP7002, capable of sampling analog signals up to 165 MHz.

The ADC receives incoming signals via dedicated physical inputs:

  • 15 kHz RGB typically enters through a SCART or component (YPbPr) connector, utilizing either composite sync (CSync), sync-on-luma, or sync-on-green.
  • 31 kHz VGA typically enters through a DE-15 connector, employing separate horizontal (HSync) and vertical (VSync) synchronization lines.

The digitizer’s internal Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) locks onto the incoming sync signals to generate a stable, phase-aligned pixel clock. Because a 15 kHz signal requires a much lower sampling clock (typically 4.5–6 MHz for standard 240p) compared to a 31 kHz signal (approximately 25–27 MHz for 480p), the ADC dynamically adjusts its sampling rate to digitize the analog waveforms into raw, uncompressed 24-bit digital RGB data.

FPGA-Based Line Multiplying

Once digitized, the pixel stream bypasses a traditional frame buffer and enters an FPGA (such as the Intel/Altera Cyclone IV). Traditional scalers write full frames into memory, analyze them, and output a new frame, creating 1 to 3 frames of input latency. The OSSC instead operates line-by-line using high-speed SRAM or embedded block RAM.

The FPGA handles the differing frequencies through line multiplication algorithms:

  • Processing 15 kHz: To make a 15 kHz (240p) signal readable by modern HDMI displays, the FPGA stores a horizontal line into a line buffer and scans it out multiple times before the next incoming line arrives. In "Line 2x" mode, each line is repeated twice, doubling the horizontal frequency from ~15 kHz to ~31 kHz, resulting in a 480p output. Higher modes like Line 3x (720p), Line 4x (960p), and Line 5x (1080p/1200p) multiply the scanlines accordingly by increasing the internal output clock rate.
  • Processing 31 kHz: Because 480p VGA signals are already operating at ~31 kHz, the FPGA does not need to double the lines to create a standard digital video signal. In "Pass-through" (Line 1x) mode, the 31 kHz signal is simply digitized and wrapped into a modern digital video transmission format without line repetition. In advanced modes like Line 2x for 480p, the FPGA multiplies the scanlines again to output 960p or 1080p, running the output pixel clock at double the speed.

Sync Recovery and Display Formatting

Modern digital displays are notoriously intolerant of out-of-spec timings and non-standard refresh rates. Standard arcade boards and older home consoles often drift slightly from the strict 59.94 Hz or 60 Hz NTSC specifications.

The FPGA continually regenerates synthetic horizontal and vertical synchronization timings to match the new output resolution while retaining the exact frame cadences of the source system. By generating standard digital video packet headers, applying optional artificial CRT scanlines, and serializing the parallel data over an HDMI transmitter, the converter delivers the digitized 15 kHz or 31 kHz signal to modern displays with less than a single scanline of input lag.
