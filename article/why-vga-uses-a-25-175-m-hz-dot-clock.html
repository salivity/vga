Why VGA Uses a 25.175 MHz Dot Clock

When IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard in 1987, it established 25.175 MHz as the dot clock frequency for standard 640-pixel wide modes. This article explains the technical reasons behind that choice, detailing how television broadcast heritage, cathode-ray tube (CRT) beam deflection physics, and horizontal line counts dictated the exact pixel clock required to deliver a stable, flicker-free 640x480 resolution at 60 Hz.

The CRT Blanking Requirement

To understand the dot clock, one must understand how analog CRT monitors function. A CRT uses an electron beam that sweeps across the screen horizontally from left to right, line by line, before returning to the top. The beam cannot jump instantly:

  • Horizontal Blanking: Time required for the beam to turn off, reposition from the right edge to the left edge (horizontal flyback), and settle before drawing the next line.
  • Vertical Blanking: Time required for the beam to turn off and travel from the bottom-right corner back to the top-left corner (vertical flyback).

Because the pixel clock keeps ticking during these invisible blanking intervals, the hardware processes more "clock cycles" per line and per frame than the visible pixel dimensions indicate.

The Frame Geometry

In standard VGA mode (640x480), the visible display is 640 pixels wide by 480 lines tall. However, the total timings include the front porch, sync pulse, and back porch:

  • Horizontal Active: 640 pixels

  • Horizontal Blanking: 160 pixels (16 front porch + 96 sync pulse + 48 back porch)

  • Total Horizontal Pixels per Line: 800 pixels

  • Vertical Active: 480 lines

  • Vertical Blanking: 45 lines (10 front porch + 2 sync pulse + 33 back porch)

  • Total Vertical Lines per Frame: 525 lines

The Legacy of NTSC and 31.5 kHz

The choice of 525 total lines was not arbitrary. It was inherited directly from the NTSC broadcast television standard, which defines a full frame as 525 lines.

Standard NTSC television used interlacing to display 525 lines at an alternating field rate of roughly 59.94 Hz (commonly referred to as 60 Hz), yielding a horizontal scan rate of roughly 15.734 kHz.

For computer monitors, interlaced text caused noticeable and fatiguing eye flicker. IBM designed VGA to be progressive (non-interlaced), drawing all 480 active lines in a single pass at ~60 Hz. To draw 525 total lines progressively at 59.94 frames per second, the monitor had to double the horizontal line frequency:

\[\text{Horizontal Scan Rate} = 525 \text{ lines} \times 59.94 \text{ Hz} \approx 31.469 \text{ kHz}\]

This horizontal frequency is commonly referred to in monitor specifications as the standard 31.5 kHz scan rate.

Calculating the 25.175 MHz Dot Clock

With 800 total clock cycles per horizontal line and a horizontal frequency of roughly 31.469 kHz, calculating the total pixel clock rate requires multiplying the horizontal line cycles by the total lines and refresh rate:

\[\text{Dot Clock} = 800 \text{ cycles/line} \times 525 \text{ lines/frame} \times 59.94 \text{ frames/second}\] \[\text{Dot Clock} = 420,000 \text{ cycles/frame} \times 59.94 \text{ frames/second} \approx 25,174,800 \text{ Hz}\]

Rounding this target frequency to match standard, mass-produced quartz crystal oscillators produced the finalized standard of 25.175 MHz.

At an exact 25.175 MHz dot clock:

  • The horizontal frequency is: \(25,175,000 / 800 = 31,468.75 \text{ Hz}\)
  • The refresh rate is: \(31,468.75 / 525 \approx 59.9405 \text{ Hz}\)

By selecting 25.175 MHz, IBM paired the visible demand for a 4:3 square-pixel 640x480 resolution with the established electronic characteristics of 525-line CRT deflection components, creating an industry baseline that endured for decades.
