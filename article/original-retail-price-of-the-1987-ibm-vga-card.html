Original Retail Price of the 1987 IBM VGA Card

When IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard in 1987, it fundamentally shifted the personal computing landscape by setting a new benchmark for visual fidelity and color depth. While VGA hardware came integrated into the motherboards of the new IBM Personal System/2 (PS/2) computer line, IBM also produced a standalone expansion board for owners of existing PC, XT, and AT computers. This article details the release history, specifications, and the original retail pricing of the standalone IBM VGA adapter in 1987.

In April 1987, IBM officially announced the standalone expansion card version of the hardware, officially marketed as the IBM PS/2 Display Adapter. The card carried an original manufacturer's suggested retail price (MSRP) of $595.

The $595 adapter used an 8-bit ISA interface, enabling backward compatibility with earlier IBM PC architectures. For this price, users received 256 kilobytes of on-board video memory, capable of delivering a 640×480 resolution in 16 colors or a 320×200 resolution in 256 colors chosen from an unprecedented hardware palette of 262,144 colors.

Upgrading to VGA in 1987 also required users to factor in the cost of a compatible display. Because VGA transitioned from digital TTL signaling to an analog RGB signal, older CGA and EGA monitors could not display the card's output. IBM offered companion analog color monitors, such as the 14-inch IBM 8512 for $595 and the higher-clarity 12-inch IBM 8513 for $685, making a complete VGA upgrade cost over $1,100 at the time of launch.

Despite the premium cost of entry, the $595 IBM PS/2 Display Adapter established the technical specifications that third-party graphics manufacturers rapidly cloned, cementing VGA as the dominant PC display standard for well over a decade.
