Aspect Ratio of 320x200 Graphics on 4:3 VGA

This article breaks down the mathematical relationship between the classic 320x200 resolution and a standard 4:3 Video Graphics Array (VGA) display. While a 320x200 pixel grid natively forms a 16:10 image, vintage cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors physically stretched this signal to fill a 4:3 screen. Understanding this transformation requires distinguishing between the storage aspect ratio, the display aspect ratio, and the resulting non-square pixel aspect ratio.

Storage Aspect Ratio (SAR)

The native resolution of 320x200 graphics represents the storage aspect ratio, which is the raw count of horizontal pixels relative to vertical pixels:

\[\text{SAR} = \frac{320}{200} = \frac{16}{10} = 1.6:1\]

If these pixels were mapped to square units (a 1:1 pixel aspect ratio), the resulting picture would produce a widescreen 16:10 format.

Display Aspect Ratio (DAR)

When output over standard VGA hardware (such as Mode 13h), the analog CRT monitor does not display the image at 16:10. Instead, the monitor's electron beam scans across the full physical frame, forcing the image into the hardware's native display aspect ratio:

\[\text{DAR} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.333:1\]

The physical picture on the screen is strictly 4:3.

Pixel Aspect Ratio (PAR)

Because a 16:10 digital buffer is compressed horizontally into a 4:3 physical area, the individual pixels cannot be square. The mathematical pixel aspect ratio (PAR) determines the width-to-height ratio of a single pixel:

\[\text{PAR} = \frac{\text{DAR}}{\text{SAR}} = \frac{4/3}{16/10} = \frac{4}{3} \times \frac{10}{16} = \frac{40}{48} = \frac{5}{6}\]

Expressed as a decimal, the horizontal-to-vertical pixel ratio is approximately \(0.8333:1\).

Inverting this ratio (\(\frac{6}{5}\)) reveals that each pixel is stretched vertically by a factor of 1.2. Consequently, graphics rendered in 320x200 on a 4:3 display appear 20% taller than their raw pixel dimensions would suggest.
