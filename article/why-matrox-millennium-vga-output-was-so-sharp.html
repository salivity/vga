Why Matrox Millennium VGA Output Was So Sharp

During the 1990s and early 2000s, Matrox Millennium graphics cards earned an industry-wide reputation for delivering the cleanest, sharpest 2D analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) signal available. While rival cards produced blurry text and smudged colors at high resolutions on CRT monitors, the Millennium series maintained immaculate clarity. This visual superiority was the direct result of premium digital-to-analog converters (RAMDACs), meticulously engineered analog filter circuits, custom high-bandwidth Window RAM (WRAM), and professional-grade printed circuit board (PCB) design.

Premium, High-Frequency RAMDACs

The primary bottleneck for analog video output in the 1990s was the Random Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converter (RAMDAC), which converts digital frame buffer data into the analog red, green, and blue voltages sent to the monitor. Most consumer graphics cards of the era used inexpensive, integrated RAMDACs with low pixel clock ceilings (often 135 MHz to 175 MHz). At higher resolutions like 1024x768 or 1280x1024 paired with ergonomic refresh rates of 75 Hz to 85 Hz, these budget DACs were pushed beyond their limits, resulting in rounded signal transitions that looked like horizontal smearing or ghosting on a CRT.

Matrox equipped the Millennium with state-of-the-art RAMDACs, such as the Texas Instruments TVP3026 on the original Millennium, capable of pixel clock speeds reaching 220 MHz. Later iterations, including the Millennium II and the G-series, pushed speeds up to 250 MHz and 360 MHz. These elevated clock ratings gave the DAC ample headroom to cleanly render steep signal transitions, preserving sharp edges around black text against white backgrounds.

Precision Analog Output Filtering

Every graphics card requires low-pass reconstruction filtering on its analog RGB lines to eliminate high-frequency radio interference (EMI) and comply with regulatory standards like FCC requirements. Many competing manufacturers implemented cheap, aggressive filter designs using budget capacitors and ferrite beads. These heavy-handed filters degraded the higher video harmonics, effectively rounding off square-wave transitions and turning sharp pixel boundaries into soft gradients.

Matrox invested heavily in precision analog engineering. Their filtering circuitry utilized tightly toleranced, high-grade passive components that carefully filtered out electromagnetic interference without attenuating the critical pixel frequencies. This transparent signal path ensured that pixel rise and fall times remained near-vertical from the DAC output all the way to the VGA port.

Dual-Ported Window RAM (WRAM)

High resolutions and refresh rates demand massive memory bandwidth. Standard single-ported DRAM or EDO RAM used by budget cards forced the memory controller to alternate between updating the screen buffer and reading data out to the display engine. When bandwidth became saturated, screen artifacts or signal timing jitter could occur.

The original Matrox Millennium circumvented this issue by using dual-ported Window RAM (WRAM), developed in partnership with Samsung. WRAM allowed simultaneous writing by the graphics processor and reading by the display controller. It also featured dedicated block-write and raster operation capabilities. This guaranteed a consistent, uninterrupted stream of pixel data to the DAC, even under heavy graphical workloads at resolutions up to 1600x1200.

Advanced PCB Layout and Noise Isolation

Analog video signals transmitted over VGA are susceptible to internal electromagnetic cross-talk generated by the host PC's motherboard, power supply, and the graphics card's own high-speed digital buses.

Matrox designed multi-layer PCBs with dedicated ground and power planes, physically separating the analog signal traces from high-frequency digital lines. The RGB traces leading to the DE-15 VGA connector were routed with matched impedance, minimizing internal reflections and standing waves that cause ghosting. By shielding the analog signal from motherboard switching noise and PCI bus traffic, Matrox delivered an exceptionally high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), rendering a steady, flicker-free picture preferred by CAD professionals, desktop publishers, and programmers.
