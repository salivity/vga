Causes of Horizontal Streaking on VGA Displays

Horizontal streaking or smearing behind high-contrast text on an analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) display is typically caused by analog signal degradation along the transmission path. Because VGA transmits video as continuous electrical voltages rather than discrete digital packets, sharp transitions—such as black text on a white background—require instantaneous voltage shifts. When hardware limitations, improper impedance, or physical cable defects prevent the signal from rising or falling rapidly, the voltage "bleeds" across the horizontal scan line, appearing on screen as visible smearing, trailing, or ghosting.

Poor Cable Quality and High Capacitance

Low-quality or excessively long VGA cables are the most common source of horizontal smearing. Inexpensive cables often lack proper coaxial shielding for the individual red, green, and blue color lines. High capacitance within the cable acts as a low-pass filter, slowing down the transition time (slew rate) between high- and low-voltage states. When the electron beam (on a CRT) or the digitizer (on an LCD) sweeps horizontally across the screen, the voltage takes too long to recover from a sharp contrast edge, causing the dark or bright pixel value to drag horizontally across the display.

Impedance Mismatches and Signal Reflections

The VGA standard specifies a strict 75-ohm characteristic impedance across the entire signal chain, including the graphics card's digital-to-analog converter (DAC), the cable, and the display's internal termination resistors. If any link in this chain deviates from 75 ohms, part of the high-frequency electrical signal bounces back toward the source and reflects again toward the monitor. These delayed reflections arrive slightly out of phase with the original signal, resulting in repeated horizontal ghosting or trailing streaks directly following high-contrast elements.

Degraded Capacitors and DC Restoration Failures

Within aging monitors and video cards, coupling capacitors are used to filter and stabilize the analog signal. Over time, electrolytic capacitors degrade and lose their original capacitance value. When input coupling capacitors fail, the circuit suffers from low-frequency phase distortion and baseline drift (poor DC restoration). Instead of stabilizing immediately after rendering a solid block of color or text, the reference voltage sags or overshoots, creating long, dark or light smears that stretch horizontally across large portions of the screen.

Insufficient Video Amplifier Bandwidth

Rendering sharp text at higher resolutions (such as 1024x768 or 1600x1200) requires a high pixel clock and substantial analog bandwidth from both the video card's output stage and the monitor’s input amplifier. If the video amplifier cannot switch fast enough to match the dot clock frequency, sharp edges become rounded. This bandwidth starvation manifests specifically as horizontal softening and directional smearing around fine, high-contrast details like typography.
