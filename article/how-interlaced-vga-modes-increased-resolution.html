How Interlaced VGA Modes Increased Resolution

Interlaced Video Graphics Array (VGA) and Super VGA (SVGA) modes allowed early computer monitors to render significantly higher resolutions than their hardware would normally support by dividing each frame into two alternating fields. Instead of drawing every horizontal line of a high-resolution image in a single pass, the graphics hardware transmitted the odd-numbered lines in one refresh cycle and the even-numbered lines in the next. This temporal multiplexing effectively halved the required video bandwidth and horizontal scanning frequency, enabling budget-friendly cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors to display sharp, pixel-dense desktops—such as 1024×768—without requiring expensive, high-frequency internal electronics.

The Bandwidth Bottleneck of Early CRTs

In a CRT monitor, an electron beam sweeps horizontally across the inside of the tube, turning on and off to illuminate phosphor dots before quickly retracing to start the next line down. The hardware constraints governing this process are video bandwidth (the pixel clock) and the horizontal scanning frequency (measured in kilohertz).

Rendering a progressive image at a resolution like 1024×768 at a standard 60 Hz refresh rate requires a horizontal scan rate of roughly 48 kHz and a pixel clock above 65 MHz. During the late 1980s and early 1990s, the deflection yokes, flyback transformers, and video amplifiers capable of operating at these frequencies were prohibitively expensive for consumer-grade displays. Standard low-cost monitors were often limited to horizontal scan rates between 31.5 kHz and 35.5 kHz, which capped native progressive displays to 640×480 or modest 800×600 modes.

How Interlacing Solved the Constraint

Interlacing bypassed these physical hardware limits by adapting a technique long used in broadcast television (such as NTSC and PAL):

  1. Field Splitting: The display controller splits a single frame into two separate fields. Field 1 contains scan lines 1, 3, 5, 7, and so on. Field 2 contains scan lines 2, 4, 6, 8, through to the bottom of the screen.
  2. Reduced Scan Lines per Pass: In a 1024×768 interlaced mode (often denoted as 1024×768i), the electron beam only draws 384 lines per vertical pass rather than all 768.
  3. Halved Line Frequency: Because the beam traverses half as many lines during each vertical refresh, the horizontal scan rate drops proportionally. For example, IBM's 8514/A and early SVGA adapters could drive 1024×768i at an 87 Hz field rate using a horizontal scan frequency of just 35.5 kHz. This was well within the operating limits of low-cost, multi-sync CRT monitors.

By lowering the horizontal scan rate, the pixel clock requirements also fell drastically. Graphics card manufacturers could use slower, cheaper digital-to-analog converters (RAMDACs) and less complex board routing while still delivering high pixel counts to the user.

Human Perception and Phosphor Persistence

Interlacing relied on two factors to produce the illusion of a complete, solid image: human persistence of vision and the decay time (persistence) of the monitor's phosphors.

Because the monitor updated half the lines every pass at high field rates (such as 87 Hz or 90 Hz), the human eye integrated the two alternating fields into a perceived complete frame rate of 43.5 Hz or 45 Hz. The glowing phosphors on the screen lingered long enough between sweeps that the odd lines had not completely faded by the time the even lines were painted, creating a stable overall image for large, uniform areas.

The Trade-Offs of the Technology

While interlaced VGA modes successfully brought high desktop resolutions to the mass market, the approach had significant drawbacks for computer applications:

  • Interline Flicker: Unlike moving television video, computer user interfaces consist of high-contrast, single-pixel horizontal lines, such as window borders, menu bars, and small text. A single horizontal line only illuminates during every other field cycle. At an effective frame rate of 43 Hz, these individual elements strobed visibly, leading to severe visual fatigue.
  • Phosphor Ghosting: To combat flicker, some monitor manufacturers used CRTs with long-persistence phosphors. While this smoothed out the strobing effect, it introduced noticeable trailing or "ghosting" when moving the mouse cursor or dragging windows.

As manufacturing techniques improved throughout the 1990s, high-frequency deflection components and fast RAMDACs became cheap to produce. This made progressive scan (non-interlaced) displays standard at 1024×768 and beyond, rendering interlaced VGA modes obsolete.
