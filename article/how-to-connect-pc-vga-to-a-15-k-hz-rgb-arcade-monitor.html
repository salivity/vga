How to Connect PC VGA to a 15 kHz RGB Arcade Monitor

Driving a vintage 15 kHz standard-resolution arcade monitor from a PC VGA output requires modifying the video signal to match arcade hardware specifications. Standard PC VGA outputs operate at a minimum horizontal scan rate of 31.5 kHz with separate horizontal and vertical sync signals at 0.7V peak-to-peak RGB levels, whereas arcade monitors strictly require roughly 15.7 kHz, composite sync (C-Sync), and often higher signal voltages (around 2V to 5V). Successfully bridging these two systems involves forcing the PC's graphics card to output a 15 kHz signal, combining the sync signals, amplifying video levels, and protecting the monitor chassis from out-of-spec frequencies.

1. Generating a 15 kHz Video Signal

Connecting a standard 31.5 kHz VGA signal directly to a 15 kHz arcade monitor can quickly destroy the monitor's horizontal output transistor (HOT) and flyback transformer. Therefore, the PC must be configured to output 15.7 kHz horizontal scan rates (240p or 480i) before connecting to the display.

  • Software Modification: CRT Emudriver (paired with compatible AMD/ATI Radeon graphics cards) is the standard software solution on Windows. It replaces standard display drivers to allow custom low-resolution modelines at native 15 kHz arcade refresh rates. On Linux, custom low-frequency modelines can be generated via xrandr or kernel mode setting (KMS).
  • Hardware Modification: Dedicated arcade graphics cards, such as the Ultimarc ArcadeVGA, natively broadcast a 15 kHz signal directly from the hardware BIOS, ensuring the PC never outputs an incompatible scan rate even during startup.

2. Combining Sync Signals (RGBHV to RGBS)

Standard VGA provides separate horizontal (H-Sync) and vertical (V-Sync) sync pulses (RGBHV) at TTL levels (5V). In contrast, most arcade monitors expect a single Composite Sync (C-Sync) signal (RGBS), typically with negative polarity.

  • Software Combining: If using CRT Emudriver, the graphics driver can often be configured via software to combine the sync signals internally, outputting pure C-Sync directly through Pin 13 (H-Sync) of the VGA port.
  • Hardware Combining: If the GPU cannot combine sync via software, a hardware circuit is required. A dedicated logic circuit utilizing a 74LS86 (XOR gate) or a 74HC08 (AND gate) reliably combines the separate horizontal and vertical TTL pulses into a clean composite sync signal. Passive circuits using resistors and diodes can work in some setups, but active logic ICs provide significantly greater stability and noise immunity.

3. Adjusting Color Signal Voltages

Standard VGA outputs analog Red, Green, and Blue signals at 0.7V peak-to-peak into a 75-ohm load. Arcade monitors (such as Wells-Gardner, Electrohome, or Nanao chassis) were designed to receive higher input levels, typically ranging from 1.0V to 5.0V peak-to-peak.

Plugging 0.7V VGA signals straight into an arcade chassis often results in a very dark or washed-out image, even with monitor contrast and brightness turned to maximum. To resolve this, install a video amplifier board (often based on high-speed operational amplifiers or video buffer chips like the THS7374) between the VGA output and the chassis input to boost the 0.7V signals to approximately 2.5V–4V.

4. Wiring and Boot Protection

To wire the modified VGA connection to the arcade monitor input header:

  • Red: Connect VGA Pin 1 to Monitor Red In.
  • Green: Connect VGA Pin 2 to Monitor Green In.
  • Blue: Connect VGA Pin 3 to Monitor Blue In.
  • Ground: Connect VGA Pins 5, 6, 7, 8, and 10 to the common Monitor Video Ground.
  • Sync: Connect the combined C-Sync line (VGA Pin 13 if driver-combined, or the output of your sync combiner) to the Monitor Sync In.

Because standard PCs output 31.5 kHz during the BIOS POST and operating system boot screen before software drivers load, an intermediary protection device is highly recommended. Commercial arcade interface boards (such as the Ultimarc J-PAC) contain frequency-detection circuitry that cuts off the sync signal if the frequency exceeds 15 kHz, preventing catastrophic chassis damage during the PC boot sequence.
