VGA Horizontal Sync Disconnected: What Happens?

Disconnecting the horizontal sync (H-sync) line on a Video Graphics Array (VGA) connection disrupts the timing signal that tells a display where each horizontal line of pixels begins. Depending on whether you are using an older cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitor or a modern flat-panel LCD display, the visual result ranges from severe horizontal tearing and diagonal distortion to an immediate blank screen showing an "Out of Range" or "No Signal" error.

VGA carries video information as an analog standard using separate pins for Red, Green, Blue, Vertical Sync (V-sync, Pin 14), and Horizontal Sync (H-sync, Pin 13). While color lines transmit brightness levels, the sync lines dictate the geometric framework of the image. The H-sync pulse specifically instructs the display’s electronics to reset its drawing path from the right side of the screen back to the left margin to begin the next scanline.

On an analog CRT monitor, cutting the H-sync signal causes the internal horizontal deflection circuit to lose its phase lock. Instead of lining up each scanline neatly above the next, the monitor's internal oscillator "free-runs" at an arbitrary frequency. This causes the image to break apart into rapid, slanted diagonal bands, jagged streaks, or a chaotic horizontal smear. If the CRT is equipped with an internal protection circuit, it may mute the electron gun entirely to prevent phosphor damage, leaving the screen completely black.

On a modern LCD or LED monitor, analog signals must first pass through an internal analog-to-digital converter (ADC) and scaler chip. These processors require a stable H-sync pulse to calculate the incoming horizontal resolution and pixel clock phase. Without an H-sync signal, the scaler cannot reconstruct the pixel grid. As a result, the monitor will not display a scrambled image; instead, it will display a message such as "No Signal," "Cable Disconnected," or "Input Signal Out of Range" before entering power-saving sleep mode.
