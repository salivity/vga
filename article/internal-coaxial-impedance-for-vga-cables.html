Internal Coaxial Impedance for VGA Cables

This article covers the electrical standards governing Video Graphics Array (VGA) cables, specifically identifying the characteristic impedance required for their internal coaxial lines. It explains why a precise impedance value is critical for preserving analog signal integrity and preventing common visual artifacts like ghosting and signal attenuation on analog displays.

The Required Characteristic Impedance

The characteristic impedance required for the internal coaxial lines within a standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) cable is 75 ohms (75 Ω).

Purpose of the 75-Ohm Standard

VGA is an analog display standard that carries separate high-frequency video signals for the primary color channels: Red, Green, and Blue (RGB). For analog video transmission to function correctly without loss of quality, the entire transmission line must be impedance-matched:

  1. Source: The digital-to-analog converter (DAC) on the graphics card outputs at a nominal impedance of 75 ohms.
  2. Medium: The internal coaxial conductors inside the VGA cable are engineered to maintain a 75-ohm characteristic impedance.
  3. Load: The display device (monitor or projector) terminates the incoming RGB lines with 75-ohm load resistors.

Internal Cable Architecture

A high-quality VGA cable uses true miniature 75-ohm coaxial lines for the three primary video signals:

  • Pin 1 (Red Video) paired with Pin 6 (Red Ground)
  • Pin 2 (Green Video) paired with Pin 7 (Green Ground)
  • Pin 3 (Blue Video) paired with Pin 8 (Blue Ground)

Each of these three lines features a central signal conductor surrounded by a dielectric insulator, a conductive braided or foil shield (which acts as the dedicated ground return), and an outer protective jacket. The physical dimensions of the conductor, the dielectric constant of the insulating material, and the shield diameter are specifically manufactured to achieve the uniform 75-ohm rating.

The remaining pins in a standard 15-pin DE-15 connector carry low-frequency signals—such as Horizontal Sync (Pin 13), Vertical Sync (Pin 14), and I2C/DDC data lines for EDID communication (Pins 12 and 15). These timing and digital communication lines do not require 75-ohm coaxial construction and typically use unshielded or standard twisted-pair wiring.

Effects of Incorrect Impedance

Deviations from the 75-ohm standard create an impedance mismatch along the transmission path. When an electrical signal reaches an interface with mismatched impedance, a portion of the signal energy reflects backward toward the source.

In an analog video system, these reflections produce distinct visual flaws:

  • Ghosting: Faint, duplicate images appearing to the right of high-contrast text or edges.
  • Ringing and Smearing: Halos, streaks, or blurry transitions between contrasting colors.
  • Signal Attenuation: Loss of brightness, color depth, or contrast, which becomes progressively worse at higher resolutions (such as 1920×1080) and longer cable distances.

Low-cost or poorly manufactured VGA cables often omit individual 75-ohm coaxial lines for the color channels, substituting them with generic unshielded twisted pairs. While these cables may function at basic resolutions like 640×480 or 800×600, maintaining a continuous 75-ohm characteristic impedance across all RGB coaxial paths is mandatory for stable, high-definition analog video output.
