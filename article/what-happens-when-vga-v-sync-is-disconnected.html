What Happens When VGA V-Sync Is Disconnected

When the vertical sync (V-sync) line of a Video Graphics Array (VGA) monitor is disconnected, the display loses the timing signal required to determine where a frame begins and ends vertically. On traditional analog CRT monitors, this disruption causes the picture to roll continuously and uncontrollably down or up the screen. On modern LCD or flat-panel monitors, the absence of this critical timing pulse typically causes the display controller to fail signal acquisition entirely, resulting in an "Out of Range," "Check Signal Cable," or "No Signal" message.

The Role of Vertical Sync in VGA

A standard VGA interface uses separate lines for video data and timing: red, green, and blue color channels carry the image brightness data, while horizontal sync (Pin 13) and vertical sync (Pin 14) regulate the scanning timing.

The vertical sync line sends a periodic pulse that tells the display to reset its electron gun or digital line buffer back to the top-left corner of the screen to begin drawing the next frame. When transmitting a 60 Hz signal, this pulse occurs 60 times per second. Without it, the monitor has horizontal information (drawing lines from left to right) but no reference for where the vertical frame boundaries lie.

Behavior on Analog CRT Monitors

On older Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitors, video is drawn directly by sweeping an electron beam across a phosphor-coated screen:

  • Vertical Rolling (Tearing): The vertical deflection circuit relies on the V-sync pulse to trigger the vertical flyback (moving the beam back to the top of the screen). Without an external pulse, the internal vertical oscillator "free-runs" at an unsynchronized frequency. Because the monitor's refresh rate and the graphics card's frame rate are no longer matched, the picture rolls rapidly and continuously across the vertical axis.
  • Partial Image Wrapping: The frame may appear split horizontally, with the bottom half of the image appearing at the top of the screen and the top half appearing at the bottom, shifting as the frequency drifts.
  • Vertical Collapse (Rare): In certain analog displays where the deflection amplifier relies entirely on an active sync pulse to oscillate, losing the vertical signal causes the vertical sweep to stop altogether, collapsing the entire picture into a single, bright, distorted horizontal line across the middle of the screen.

Behavior on Digital LCD and Flat-Panel Monitors

Unlike CRTs, modern LCD and LED displays do not draw images directly via an electron beam. Instead, an internal scalar chip converts the incoming analog VGA signal into a digital frame buffer:

  • "No Signal" or "Out of Range" Error: The digital scaler requires both horizontal and vertical sync pulses to lock onto a valid resolution and refresh rate (such as 1024x768 at 60 Hz). If Pin 14 is disconnected, the timing parameters become invalid. Most modern monitors will refuse to display the corrupted image and will shut off the backlight or display an on-screen warning stating that the input signal is missing or unsupported.
  • Flickering Blank Screen: If the monitor attempts to interpret the signal without V-sync, it will intermittently flash black, cycle through power-saving modes, or display erratic, jumping horizontal blocks before going into standby.
