1280x1024 VGA Standard: Understanding SXGA

The 1280x1024 display resolution corresponds to the SXGA (Super Extended Graphics Array) standard. As computer display technologies progressed beyond the original Video Graphics Array (VGA) specifications, manufacturers created standard naming conventions to designate higher pixel counts. SXGA represents a significant milestone in this lineage, uniquely combining high pixel density with a 5:4 aspect ratio that set it apart from other contemporary standards.

The VGA Hierarchy and SXGA

The Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard, introduced by IBM in 1987, originally defined a resolution of 640x480 pixels. As monitor technology improved, standardized acronyms were established to describe resolutions that scaled up from the base VGA architecture:

  • VGA: 640 x 480 (4:3 aspect ratio)
  • SVGA (Super VGA): 800 x 600 (4:3 aspect ratio)
  • XGA (Extended Graphics Array): 1024 x 768 (4:3 aspect ratio)
  • SXGA (Super Extended Graphics Array): 1280 x 1024 (5:4 aspect ratio)

Following this progression, SXGA increased the total addressable pixels to approximately 1.31 million, delivering significantly sharper text and images than earlier standards.

The Unique 5:4 Aspect Ratio

Most graphics standards derived from VGA maintained a traditional 4:3 aspect ratio. A true 4:3 counterpart to 1024 vertical lines would have been 1365 or 1280x960 (known as SXGA- or UVGA).

Instead, SXGA standardized on 1280x1024, resulting in a 5:4 aspect ratio. Because this resolution was slightly narrower than standard 4:3 CRT monitors, displaying 1280x1024 on an older CRT screen caused non-square pixels unless the monitor's geometry controls were manually adjusted.

Adoption and Common Uses

Despite the unusual aspect ratio, SXGA became the native resolution for standard 17-inch and 19-inch LCD desktop monitors throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. The 5:4 ratio provided extra vertical space, making it exceptionally popular for office productivity, word processing, software development, and industrial displays before widescreen formats (such as 16:9 and 16:10) became the global standard.
