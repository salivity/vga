Which VGA Pin Was Originally Removed?

The standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) connector features a three-row, 15-pin layout known as the DE-15 connector, but Pin 9 was originally omitted or blocked to act as a physical key. This article explains which pin served as the VGA key pin, why it was removed, and how its function eventually changed with newer display standards.

The Keyed Pin: Pin 9

In the original IBM VGA specification introduced in 1987, Pin 9 was removed from the male plug and the corresponding hole on the female socket was plugged. This design choice created a physical "keying" mechanism.

Purpose of Keying Pin 9

The removal of Pin 9 served two primary purposes:

  • Preventing Misalignment and Incorrect Insertion: The blocked hole ensured that only compatible VGA cables could be connected to VGA ports, preventing users from attempting to force incompatible 15-pin connectors into the display port.
  • Distinguishing Port Types: During the late 1980s, several computing peripherals used similar D-subminiature connectors. Keying the port helped avoid accidental connections to non-VGA hardware, protecting both the monitor and the graphics card from electrical damage.

The Evolution of Pin 9

As display technology evolved, the physical keying of Pin 9 became obsolete:

  • VESA DDC Introduction: In the mid-1990s, the Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) introduced the Display Data Channel (DDC) standard. This standard required the monitor and the graphics adapter to communicate display capabilities, such as supported resolutions and refresh rates, via Extended Display Identification Data (EDID).
  • +5V DC Power Delivery: To power the EDID EEPROM chip inside the monitor—allowing the computer to read display data even when the monitor was turned off—Pin 9 was restored and repurposed to supply +5V DC power.
  • Modern VGA Cables: Modern VGA cables and ports feature all 15 pins fully populated and unblocked, rendering the original mechanical keying a relic of early PC hardware design.
