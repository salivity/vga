Why IBM PS/2 Integrated VGA on the Planar

When IBM introduced the Personal System/2 (PS/2) in 1987, it radically altered PC hardware design by soldering the newly developed Video Graphics Array (VGA) directly onto the system planar (motherboard). This article examines the strategic, technical, and economic factors behind that decision, detailing how IBM leveraged Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) to reduce manufacturing costs, preserve valuable expansion slots, counter the rise of PC clone manufacturers, and establish an immediate graphics baseline across its entire product line.

Reducing Manufacturing and Component Costs

Prior to the PS/2, displaying high-resolution graphics required expensive dedicated video adapters, such as the Enhanced Graphics Adapter (EGA) card, which contained dozens of discrete logic chips. By 1987, IBM had developed custom VLSI gate arrays that condensed the entirety of the VGA hardware—including the controller, sequencer, attribute controller, and digital-to-analog converter (DAC)—into a single primary chip and a minimal RAMDAC pairing.

Because the chip count dropped so drastically, surface-mounting the VGA circuitry directly onto the planar was cheaper than producing, packaging, testing, and installing a separate add-in daughtercard. It streamlined assembly on IBM’s highly automated robotic production lines, reducing production errors and physical materials.

Conserving Micro Channel Architecture (MCA) Slots

The flagship models of the PS/2 series (such as the Model 50, 60, and 80) debuted the proprietary Micro Channel Architecture (MCA) bus. To achieve smaller desktop footprints, models like the Model 50 provided only three MCA expansion slots.

If video had required a dedicated card—alongside standard add-ins for networking, memory, or SCSI storage—users would have exhausted expansion capacity immediately. Integrating video, along with the floppy drive controller, serial ports, parallel port, and PS/2 mouse interface directly on the motherboard ensured that all available expansion slots remained open for aftermarket expansion.

Fighting IBM PC Clones

By the mid-1980s, "clone" manufacturers such as Compaq and Phoenix Technologies had successfully cloned the original IBM PC/AT architecture and were undercutting IBM's market share. IBM designed the PS/2 to regain market control through proprietary technology.

Integrating VGA onto the planar via proprietary IBM Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) made PS/2 motherboards difficult and legally hazardous to copy. A competitor could not simply bundle a generic motherboard with third-party video cards to achieve the same form factor and integrated experience. IBM held patents on both the VGA silicon and its interaction with the planar bus, forcing clone makers to reverse-engineer complex analog-digital circuitry before they could catch up.

Establishing an Unfragmented Graphics Standard

Before VGA, the PC ecosystem was fragmented across MDA, CGA, EGA, Hercules, and proprietary display standards. Software developers routinely struggled to support multiple display adapters with varying color palettes and resolutions.

By soldering VGA onto the motherboard of every mainstream PS/2 system, IBM ensured that 100% of these machines supported 640×480 resolution at 16 colors and 320×200 at 256 colors natively, alongside analog RGB monitors. This immediate ubiquity forced software vendors to adopt VGA as the standard target for PC gaming and graphical user interfaces, such as Windows and OS/2.

System Bus Efficiency and Integration

Direct planar integration allowed IBM engineers to route video signals and bus arbitration logic with shorter circuit traces, minimizing electromagnetic interference and bus latency compared to passing signals through a physical slot connector. It also allowed seamless support for the PS/2 Auxiliary Video Connector—an internal feature allowing specialized add-on cards to intercept or overlay planar video output without disabling the primary integrated display.
