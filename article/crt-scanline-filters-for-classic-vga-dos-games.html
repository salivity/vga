CRT Scanline Filters for Classic VGA DOS Games

Scanline filters recreate the warm, textured visual presentation of 1990s cathode-ray tube (CRT) computer monitors for modern players running classic Video Graphics Array (VGA) DOS titles. By emulating the physical characteristics of analog displays—such as horizontal beam gaps, phosphor glow, and optical blending—these shaders soften the harsh, razor-sharp edges produced by modern flat panels. Ultimately, scanline filters restore the cohesive visual aesthetic that original DOS game artists specifically designed their pixel art to achieve.

Emulating CRT Double-Scanning and Beam Gaps

Classic DOS games running in standard VGA Mode 13h featured a low native resolution of 320x200 pixels. Because standard VGA monitors operated at a minimum vertical refresh rate that required 400 lines of video signal, the display hardware drew each scanline twice.

Scanline filters simulate the visible dark intervals between these active electron beam passes. Rather than displaying uniform, blocky squares, the screen is broken into horizontal bands. This creates a tactile, woven texture across the image that prevents sprites and background art from looking like flat, enlarged digital tiles.

Natural Dithering and Color Blending

Due to the 256-color limit of standard VGA graphics, artists relied heavily on "dithering"—placing contrasting pixels in checkerboard patterns to imply intermediate shades, shadows, and smooth gradients.

On a modern LCD, dithered patterns appear jarring and distinct. A scanline filter, often combined with a light bloom or subpixel mask shader, replicates the analog bleed of CRT phosphors. The light from adjacent pixels subtly merges across the scanlines, turning harsh checkerboards into smooth lighting transitions, rich skin tones, and soft environmental shading as originally intended.

The Illusion of Perceived Detail

Sharp, raw pixels on high-resolution displays exaggerate the low fidelity of 320x200 artwork, exposing hard borders and jagged edges. The rhythmic spacing introduced by simulated scanlines acts as a natural anti-aliasing filter. By interrupting solid vertical blocks of color, scanlines trick the human eye into perceiving finer detail, giving character portraits, UI elements, and 3D wireframes or textured polygons greater apparent depth and definition.

Restoring Developer Intent

Game developers throughout the late 1980s and 1990s crafted their artwork exclusively on analog CRT monitors, actively relying on the natural imperfections of the glass screen, shadow masks, and scanlines to finish the image. Implementing a scanline filter does not merely trigger nostalgia; it corrects the visual distortion caused by modern fixed-pixel screens, presenting classic DOS titles through the optical framework for which they were built.
