How Multisync Monitors Handled VGA Frequencies

This article examines how multisync (multiscan) monitors adapted to the flexible timing requirements of the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard. Before multisync technology, computer displays were hardwired to strict, singular scan rates. The introduction of VGA and its higher-resolution successors required displays to actively detect, lock onto, and scale across a wide range of horizontal and vertical scan frequencies without burning out internal components. Below is an explanation of the technical mechanisms—from signal detection and phase-locked loops to dynamic deflection circuitry and microprocessor memory—that made this adaptability possible.

The Challenge of Variable VGA Timings

Early personal computer display standards, such as IBM’s MDA, CGA, and EGA, operated at fixed horizontal frequencies (roughly 18.43 kHz, 15.75 kHz, and 21.8 kHz, respectively). Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitors designed for these standards utilized rigid deflection circuits tuned to resonate at a single frequency.

In 1987, IBM introduced the VGA standard, which required a baseline horizontal frequency of 31.469 kHz and supported multiple refresh rates, such as 60 Hz for 640x480 graphics and 70 Hz for 720x400 text mode. As Super VGA (SVGA) emerged, horizontal frequencies soared anywhere from 31 kHz to beyond 100 kHz, with vertical refresh rates varying between 56 Hz and 120 Hz. Feeding these changing frequencies into a fixed-frequency monitor caused deflection failure and permanent hardware damage, necessitating a dynamic solution: the multisync monitor.

Sync Pulse Detection and Separation

Multisync monitors adapted to diverse signals beginning at the input stage. Unlike earlier standards that often mixed synchronization information into the video line (composite sync), the standard 15-pin VGA connector provided dedicated lines for Horizontal Sync (H-Sync) and Vertical Sync (V-Sync).

Inside the monitor, a sync processing circuit monitored these incoming TTL (transistor-transistor logic) pulses:

  1. Polarity Detection: VGA used the polarity of H-Sync and V-Sync pulses (positive or negative) to signal the intended vertical resolution (e.g., 350, 400, or 480 lines). Multisync circuitry read these polarities to anticipate deflection needs.
  2. Frequency Counting: An internal frequency counter measured the duration between pulses to determine the exact horizontal line rate and vertical frame rate.

Phase-Locked Loops (PLL) and Horizontal Oscillators

Once the monitor detected the incoming sync rates, it relied on Phase-Locked Loops (PLLs) to synchronize its internal scan generation with the graphics card.

The incoming horizontal sync pulse acted as the reference frequency for the PLL. The PLL compared the phase and frequency of the input signal to a feedback signal generated by the monitor’s internal horizontal oscillator. Any difference generated an error voltage that adjusted the internal oscillator until it perfectly matched the incoming signal. This continuous feedback loop allowed multisync monitors to track smooth variations or entirely switch between different horizontal frequencies on the fly.

Relay-Switched and Wide-Range Deflection Circuits

Driving a CRT requires pushing high electrical currents through the horizontal and vertical deflection yokes to steer the electron beam across the screen. The horizontal deflection circuit is particularly sensitive, as the energy required scales directly with frequency. Multisync monitors accommodated wide frequency spans using two primary methods:

  • Switched Capacitance and B+ Voltage Regulation: Early multisync monitors divided frequencies into discrete ranges. When the monitor detected a jump from standard VGA (31.5 kHz) to SVGA (38 kHz or 48 kHz), relays or solid-state MOSFET switches would swap in different tuning capacitors and adjust the "B+" supply voltage fed to the flyback transformer and horizontal output transistor (HOT). This prevented the horizontal output stage from overheating or under-driving.
  • Continuous Resonant Tracking: Later, fully variable designs used sophisticated switched-mode power supplies that scaled the rail voltage continuously to match the calculated horizontal frequency, eliminating the need for mechanical relays.

Dynamic Geometric Correction and Digital Control

As scan rates change, the magnetic forces required to maintain an undistorted raster image also shift. Increasing the horizontal frequency without adjusting the vertical sweep leads to stretched, squashed, or geometrically distorted images (such as pincushioning, trapezoid errors, and barrel distortion).

Analog multisync designs used dynamic correction circuits that tapped into the horizontal and vertical deflection feedback to modulate yoke current in real time.

By the early 1990s, monitors integrated onboard microcontrollers paired with non-volatile EEPROM memory. When a user adjusted the screen size, centering, and geometry for an uncommon resolution, the microcontroller mapped these settings to the specific H-Sync and V-Sync signature. When that frequency combination was detected again, the monitor automatically recalled the stored calibration values, allowing seamless transitions between DOS text prompts, standard VGA gaming, and high-resolution desktop environments.
