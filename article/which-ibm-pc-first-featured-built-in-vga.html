Which IBM PC First Featured Built-in VGA?

The IBM Personal System/2 (PS/2) family, launched in April 1987, was the first computer line to feature the Video Graphics Array (VGA) directly integrated onto the motherboard. Designed to replace the older PC, XT, and AT architectures, the PS/2 introduced VGA as a major leap forward in personal computer display technology. This standard quickly displaced earlier configurations like CGA and EGA, establishing the 640x480 resolution and 256-color palette that served as the foundational visual benchmark for the PC industry for more than a decade.

The Debut of the IBM Personal System/2

When IBM announced the PS/2 line on April 2, 1987, it represented an aggressive attempt to regain control of the personal computer market from clone manufacturers. While the line introduced proprietary designs like the Micro Channel Architecture (MCA) bus, its graphics hardware became an industry-wide triumph.

Rather than requiring an add-in expansion board, higher-tier models such as the PS/2 Model 50, Model 60, and Model 80 shipped with VGA circuitry built directly onto the planar (motherboard). The lower-end PS/2 models, specifically the Model 25 and Model 30, featured a scaled-down version called the Multi-Color Graphics Array (MCGA), but subsequent models rapidly standardized full VGA implementation across the platform.

Why VGA Was a Milestone in Display Technology

Prior to VGA, IBM-compatible PCs relied primarily on the Color Graphics Adapter (CGA) and the Enhanced Graphics Adapter (EGA). These standards offered limited color selections and required digital TTL monitors, which constrained the intensity and range of shades available on screen.

VGA transformed PC graphics by moving from digital to analog video signaling. This shift enabled several critical advancements:

  • Massive Color Palette: VGA utilized an internal digital-to-analog converter (RAMDAC) that supported an 18-bit color space, yielding a master palette of 262,144 distinct colors.
  • Versatile Display Modes: Its headline modes included 640x480 resolution with 16 simultaneous colors for detailed productivity work, and 320x200 resolution with 256 simultaneous colors (Mode 13h), which revolutionized PC gaming.
  • Standardized 15-Pin Connector: The PS/2 introduced the high-density DE-15 connector, colloquially known as the VGA port, which remained the universal standard for computer monitors and projectors well into the 2000s.
  • Crisper Text Reproduction: With an 8x16 or 9x16 character cell matrix, text mode clarity vastly improved, making command-line navigation and early word processors far easier on the eyes.

The Transition from Proprietary Feature to Global Standard

Although IBM intended the PS/2 platform to create a closed ecosystem that competitors could not easily replicate, third-party hardware manufacturers swiftly reverse-engineered the VGA chipsets. Companies such as Cirrus Logic, Tseng Labs, and Western Digital developed independent VGA and "Super VGA" (SVGA) controllers that could be installed in standard ISA expansion slots.

Consequently, while the PS/2 hardware line itself eventually lost ground to cheaper, open-standard PC clones, the built-in VGA technology it debuted survived and became the universal foundation for personal computer graphics for decades to follow.
