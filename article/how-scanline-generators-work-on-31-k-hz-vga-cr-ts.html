How Scanline Generators Work on 31 kHz VGA CRTs

An active scanline generator simulates the appearance of low-resolution retro displays on high-resolution 31 kHz CRT monitors by hardware-intercepting analog video signals and darkening alternate horizontal lines. This article explains how these devices track synchronization pulses, use digital switching logic to alternate between odd and even lines, and manipulate analog RGB voltage levels to recreate authentic retro scanlines without software emulation.

The 15 kHz vs. 31 kHz Disconnect

Standard-definition retro gaming consoles (such as the NES, Genesis, or SNES) output video at roughly 15 kHz, commonly known as 240p. Because 240p only draws every other field without interlacing, thick blank gaps—scanlines—naturally appear between drawn lines on standard 15 kHz CRT televisions.

In contrast, standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) PC monitors operate at a horizontal refresh rate of 31 kHz (480p). When upscaling or displaying 480p content, the monitor draws all 480 progressive lines consecutively, eliminating those characteristic black gaps. An active scanline generator bridges this visual gap by artificially suppressing every other horizontal sweep in hardware.

Synchronization Detection and Line Counting

Active scanline generators sit inline between the video source and the display via standard VGA (DE-15) connections. To determine which lines to darken, the device monitors two discrete signals:

  1. Vertical Sync (VSYNC): Signals the start of a new video frame. When the VSYNC pulse arrives, the generator's internal logic counter resets to a defined starting state (line 0).
  2. Horizontal Sync (HSYNC): Signals the end of one horizontal line and the start of the next. Each HSYNC pulse advances an internal logic circuit—often built using high-speed 7400-series logic (such as a D or T flip-flop) or a small complex programmable logic device (CPLD).

By toggling on every HSYNC pulse, the flip-flop's output alternates between high (logic 1) and low (logic 0) for odd and even scanlines across the 480-line frame.

Signal Attenuation on Alternating Lines

Once the generator identifies an alternating line, it manipulates the analog color channels:

  • Pass-Through State: On the designated visible line, high-speed analog switches (such as CMOS multiplexers or fast switching transistors) allow the Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) signals to pass unaltered from the input to the output pins at the standard 0.7V peak-to-peak amplitude.
  • Darkened State: On the alternate line, the logic circuit switches the RGB pathways through an attenuation circuit. Depending on the user's settings, the generator either ties the RGB signals directly to ground via an internal load (creating completely black scanlines) or routes the signal through variable resistors/potentiometers. Bleeding a portion of the RGB signal voltage to ground reduces line brightness to 25%, 50%, or 75%, generating variable-opacity scanlines.

Why Active Processing Matters

Passive scanline generators rely solely on line power stolen from the VGA cable, which can result in video ghosting, impedance mismatching, and overall picture dimming.

Active scanline generators use an independent power source (typically 5V via micro-USB or pin 9 on the VGA connector) to drive low-latency logic gates and operational buffers. This dedicated power allows the analog switches to toggle within nanoseconds during the horizontal blanking interval, preventing color bleed, preserving signal impedance (75 ohms), and keeping non-darkened lines at full, vibrant brightness.
