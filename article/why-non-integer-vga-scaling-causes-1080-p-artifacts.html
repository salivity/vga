Why Non-Integer VGA Scaling Causes 1080p Artifacts

Displaying a standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) signal on a 1080p monitor produces uneven pixel artifacts because the source resolution cannot be divided evenly into the display's native resolution. Modern flat-panel monitors rely on fixed grids of physical pixels, requiring incoming low-resolution signals to be digitally multiplied to fill the screen. Because the scale factor between standard 480p VGA and 1080p is a fractional number (2.25x), individual source pixels cannot map symmetrically onto physical screen pixels, resulting in distortion, inconsistent line thickness, and visual shimmering.

The Mathematical Mismatch

Standard VGA outputs a resolution of 640x480 pixels in a 4:3 aspect ratio. A standard 1080p screen has a native resolution of 1920x1080 pixels (16:9).

When mapping a 4:3 image correctly onto a 1080p screen with proper proportions, the display uses a 1440x1080 area (pillarboxed with black bars on the sides):

  • Horizontal: 1440 ÷ 640 = 2.25
  • Vertical: 1080 ÷ 480 = 2.25

Even if the image is stretched horizontally to fill the full 1920 pixels (distorting the aspect ratio), the vertical axis remains problematic:

  • Horizontal: 1920 ÷ 640 = 3.0 (an integer scale)
  • Vertical: 1080 ÷ 480 = 2.25 (a non-integer scale)

Because physical LCD and OLED pixels are indivisible physical units, a screen cannot illuminate a quarter (0.25) of a pixel. Every original pixel must somehow be distributed across a non-whole number of physical display elements.

How Display Scalers Handle Fractional Math

When a scaler attempts to fit a 1-pixel-wide line across 2.25 physical pixels, it generally relies on one of two scaling methods, each producing distinct artifacts:

1. Nearest-Neighbor Scaling

With nearest-neighbor scaling, the display duplicates pixels to approximate the required size without blending colors. To achieve a 2.25x average scale, the display must alternate how many physical pixels represent a source pixel. Over a four-pixel span:

  • Pixel 1 is drawn across 2 physical pixels.
  • Pixel 2 is drawn across 2 physical pixels.
  • Pixel 3 is drawn across 2 physical pixels.
  • Pixel 4 is drawn across 3 physical pixels.

This creates a visible pattern where every fourth pixel or line is 50% wider or taller than the others. In video games or moving graphics, this uneven distribution causes scrolling objects to warp, jitter, or "shimmer" as pixels dynamically expand and contract across the screen.

2. Bilinear or Interpolated Scaling

To hide the abrupt size differences of nearest-neighbor scaling, many monitors apply linear interpolation (blurring). The scaler calculates intermediate colors for the fractional borders by averaging adjacent pixels. While this removes the harsh geometric unevenness, it replaces sharp pixel edges with uneven smearing and softness, making text hard to read and retro graphics appear out of focus.

Analog Sampling Irregularities

Because standard VGA transmits an analog waveform rather than digital data packets, the monitor must first digitize the incoming signal using an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). If the monitor's sampling clock does not perfectly match the phase and pixel clock of the VGA output, the digitized pixels will already exhibit slight phase noise and uneven widths. When this imperfectly sampled 480p grid is subsequently scaled by 2.25x, the phase errors are magnified, further exaggerating the uneven pixel artifacts.

The Clean Solution: Integer Scaling

The only way to avoid uneven scaling artifacts is to enforce integer scaling. By scaling the 640x480 image by an exact integer factor of 2x (yielding 1280x960), every single source pixel is mapped cleanly to a uniform 2x2 grid of four physical pixels. While this leaves an unused border of black pixels around the 1080p display, it completely eliminates distortion, uneven pixel widths, and motion shimmering.
