How CRT Shaders Emulate VGA Bloom and Phosphor Decay

Modern emulators rely on advanced multi-pass fragment shaders to reproduce the physical characteristics of classic cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors. When running retro Video Graphics Array (VGA) games, these shaders simulate hardware-level phenomena such as electron beam bloom and phosphor persistence. By mathematically modeling how high-energy electron beams scatter across shadow masks and how phosphors slowly release energy, shaders transform raw, blocky digital pixels into the soft, luminous images originally envisioned by DOS-era developers.

Recreating Electron Beam Bloom

Beam bloom occurs when an electron gun emits high currents to display bright colors, exciting the screen's phosphors beyond their standard focus area and widening the scanning beam. Shaders recreate this interaction through dynamic scanline modulation and luminance halation.

  1. Scanline Beam Widening: Standard CRT shaders calculate the luminance of each incoming pixel. For darker colors, the simulated electron beam remains focused, leaving prominent dark gaps (scanlines) between raster rows. For brighter values, the shader dynamically expands the vertical profile of the beam, causing bright scanlines to swell and partially fill the neighboring dark gaps.
  2. Multi-Pass Halation and Glow: To simulate light bleeding through the monitor's front glass and internal reflections, shaders use multi-pass blur algorithms. The shader extracts bright pixel data above a set threshold, runs it through a series of Gaussian downsampling and upsampling passes, and composites this softened light back over the original image. This creates a natural optical halo around high-contrast elements, such as white text on a dark background.

Simulating Phosphor Decay and Persistence

Phosphor decay, or persistence, is the brief duration a CRT’s phosphor coating continues emitting photons after the electron beam has moved on. Standard VGA monitors used P22 phosphors, which had distinct decay rates for red, green, and blue sub-pixels. Shaders mimic this temporal effect using frame history buffers:

  • Feedback Loops: The shader architecture preserves the rendered output of the previous frame in an off-screen texture buffer.

  • Exponential Decay Mathematics: Rather than replacing the old frame entirely, the shader blends a fraction of the buffered frame into the current frame. The decay follows an exponential falloff function:

    \[\text{Color}_{\text{final}} = \text{Color}_{\text{current}} + (\text{Color}_{\text{previous}} \times e^{-k \cdot \Delta t})\]

    In this formula, \(k\) represents the decay constant and \(\Delta t\) represents elapsed frame time.

  • Per-Channel Latency: Advanced shaders assign different decay values to each color channel. Because green phosphors typically exhibit longer persistence than blue or red phosphors, the shader retains green values slightly longer across subsequent frames. This produces authentic phosphor trails and eliminates modern LCD motion blur by emulating natural CRT motion smoothing.

Adapting to the VGA Standard

Unlike consumer televisions that operated at a 15 kHz horizontal frequency, standard VGA monitors operated at 31.5 kHz. At lower resolutions, such as the 320×200 mode common in early PC gaming, the monitor double-scanned the signal to produce 400 lines at 70 Hz.

CRT shaders specifically tuned for VGA emulate this tighter line pitch and higher refresh rate. The simulated shadow masks and aperture grilles feature much higher dot densities, resulting in sharper scanlines with narrower bloom radiuses than those designed for home game consoles. By integrating precise luminance bloom with temporal decay buffers, shaders deliver the high-contrast, luminous look inherent to vintage PC hardware.
