1600x1200 Resolution: UXGA Standard Explained

The 1600x1200 display resolution is formally designated as UXGA, which stands for Ultra Extended Graphics Array (or Ultra Video Graphics Array). This article details the UXGA naming standard, its technical relationship to the foundational Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard, its core specifications, and its practical applications in computing display history.

The Naming Standard: UXGA

Relative to the original Video Graphics Array standard, a resolution of 1600x1200 pixels is designated as UXGA (Ultra Extended Graphics Array). It represents a direct scaling of the standard 4:3 computer display progression established in the late 1980s and 1990s.

The lineage builds incrementally from the baseline standard:

  • VGA (Video Graphics Array): 640x480
  • SVGA (Super VGA): 800x600
  • XGA (Extended Graphics Array): 1024x768
  • SXGA (Super XGA): 1280x1024 (Note: 5:4 ratio)
  • UXGA (Ultra XGA): 1600x1200

Relationship to Standard VGA

UXGA maintains the exact 4:3 aspect ratio introduced by standard VGA (640x480), but with significantly higher pixel density:

  • Horizontal Resolution: 1600 pixels (2.5 times the 640 pixels of standard VGA)
  • Vertical Resolution: 1200 pixels (2.5 times the 480 pixels of standard VGA)
  • Total Pixel Count: 1,920,000 pixels (1.92 megapixels), which is exactly 6.25 times the 307,200 pixels of a standard VGA screen.

Because UXGA scales both axes by a factor of 2.5, it provides significantly more screen real estate while preserving the traditional fullscreen geometric proportions required by legacy software designed for standard VGA.

Key Applications and Use Cases

UXGA was widely adopted during the peak of high-end CRT monitors and early professional-grade LCDs throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. Key applications include:

  • Professional Workstations: Graphic designers, CAD engineers, and software developers favored UXGA 20-inch and 21-inch monitors for high vertical workspace before widescreen monitors became standard.
  • Medical Imaging: Diagnostic displays, such as PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System) monitors, utilized UXGA panels in both landscape and portrait orientations for high-clarity X-ray and scan viewing.
  • High-End Laptops: Premium mobile workstations in the mid-2000s featured 15-inch UXGA screens, offering some of the sharpest pixel densities available at the time.
