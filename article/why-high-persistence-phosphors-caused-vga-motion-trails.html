Why High-Persistence Phosphors Caused VGA Motion Trails

When fast-paced Video Graphics Array (VGA) games emerged, players on certain displays frequently noticed distracting motion trails—often called ghosting—behind moving objects. This visual smearing was directly caused by high-persistence phosphors, which are chemical compounds on the screen that take a relatively long time to stop glowing after being stimulated. While useful for static displays and low-refresh-rate terminals, these slow-decaying phosphors could not clear the previous frame's image fast enough to keep up with the rapid frame rates and dynamic action of early PC games.

To understand why this happened, it is necessary to examine how cathode-ray tube (CRT) and early display technologies produced an image. Inside a CRT, an electron gun fires focused beams across the screen in a rapid scanning pattern. The inside face of the screen is coated with phosphors—chemical substances that fluoresce, or emit light, when struck by electrons. The time it takes for this emitted light to fade away to an imperceptible level is known as phosphor persistence.

Phosphors were engineered with varying persistence times depending on the intended use of the monitor:

  • High-persistence phosphors were originally selected for radar screens, monochrome terminals, and early business monitors operating at low refresh rates (often 30 to 50 Hz). The slow decay time kept the pixels glowing between electron scans, which effectively eliminated eye-straining screen flicker for static text and spreadsheets.
  • Short-persistence phosphors decayed within fractions of a millisecond, making them suitable for fast-changing imagery where frames needed to disappear instantly to prepare for the next scan.

The conflict arose when the IBM VGA standard popularized high-color, fast-action video games operating at standard refresh rates of 60 Hz or 70 Hz. In games featuring rapid side-scrolling, high-speed projectiles, or fast first-person movement, individual pixels needed to transition from bright to dark in roughly 14 to 16 milliseconds.

When a display utilized high-persistence phosphors, the decay time far exceeded the duration of a single frame. As a bright sprite or background moved across the screen, the electron beam moved to excite a new group of phosphors on the subsequent pass. However, the phosphors at the object's previous location continued to glow brightly. Because the human eye perceived both the new position of the object and the lingering afterglow of its old position simultaneously, the moving graphic appeared to drag a luminous tail or smear across the screen.

The issue eventually resolved as display manufacturers transitioned to medium-short persistence phosphor formulations, such as the standard P22 phosphor family. These faster-decaying compounds allowed each frame to extinguish rapidly, restoring crisp, smear-free motion to high-speed VGA gaming.
