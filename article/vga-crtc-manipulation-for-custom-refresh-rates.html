VGA CRTC Manipulation for Custom Refresh Rates

During the golden era of PC demoscene coding, programmers bypassed standard BIOS video interrupts to directly program the Video Graphics Array (VGA) hardware via its I/O ports. By modifying the internal registers of the Cathode Ray Tube Controller (CRTC), coders adjusted horizontal and vertical timing parameters to produce non-standard refresh rates such as 50 Hz, 60 Hz, or even 100 Hz instead of the default 70 Hz found in standard Mode 13h. This article explains the register mechanics, lock states, and timing calculations used by demoscene coders to override fixed display rates for smoother animations and optimized raster budgets.

The CRTC Architecture and Port Access

The VGA hardware exposes the CRTC registers through an indexed I/O port scheme. Depending on the configuration of the Miscellaneous Output Register (port 0x3C2), the CRTC registers are mapped to either monochrome (0x3B4/0x3B5) or color I/O ports (0x3D4/0x3D5). In standard color graphics modes:

  • Port 0x3D4 acts as the Index Register, receiving the register number (0x00 to 0x18).
  • Port 0x3D5 acts as the Data Register, allowing read and write operations for the selected register.

Unlocking the Protected Registers

IBM designed the VGA CRTC with a hardware write-protect mechanism to prevent software from sending out-of-range scan frequencies that could physically damage sensitive CRT monitors. Registers 0x00 through 0x07—which contain critical horizontal and vertical timing definitions—are write-protected upon system boot.

To bypass this protection, coders cleared Bit 7 of the Vertical Retrace End Register (CRTC index 0x11). The unlocking sequence required a direct write:

mov dx, 0x3D4
mov al, 0x11
out dx, al
inc dx
in  al, dx
and al, 0x7F        ; Clear bit 7 to enable write access to CRTC 0x00-0x07
out dx, al

The Math of VGA Refresh Rates

A CRT monitor traces an electron beam from left to right (horizontal scan) and top to bottom (vertical sweep). The total frame rate (refresh rate) is strictly governed by the dot clock, the total number of horizontal clocks per scanline, and the total number of scanlines per frame:

\[\text{Refresh Rate} = \frac{\text{Dot Clock}}{\text{Horizontal Total} \times \text{Vertical Total}}\]

The VGA provides two standard base pixel clocks via port 0x3C2: 25.175 MHz (used for 320/640-pixel modes) and 28.322 MHz (used for 360/720-pixel text modes). Because the pixel clock oscillator frequencies were fixed on standard hardware, altering the refresh rate required manipulating the Horizontal Total and Vertical Total register fields.

Modifying Vertical Timing for Target Frequencies

While modifying horizontal timing changed line rates, modifying vertical timing was the primary method for altering frame rates. Vertical counters on the VGA are 10-bit values split across primary registers and the Overflow Register (index 0x07):

  • Vertical Total (Index 0x06): Sets bits 0–7 of the total scanline count per frame (including active lines, porches, and vertical retrace). Bits 8 and 9 are located in index 0x07 (bits 0 and 5).
  • Vertical Display End (Index 0x12): Sets the number of visible scanlines rendered before the vertical blanking interval begins.
  • Vertical Retrace Start (Index 0x10): Defines the scanline where the monitor’s vertical synchronization pulse is fired.
  • Vertical Retrace End (Index 0x11): Controls the pulse width of the vertical sync signal.

Standard Mode 13h used a vertical total of 449 scanlines with a 25.175 MHz dot clock divided by a horizontal total of 800 clock cycles (400 display clocks in doubled modes), resulting in approximately 70.08 Hz:

\[\frac{25,175,000}{800 \times 449} \approx 70.08\text{ Hz}\]

Shifting to 60 Hz or 50 Hz

Demoscene coders targeting smooth 60 Hz playback (common for NTSC compatibility) or 50 Hz (preferred for European PAL systems and conversions from the Amiga) increased the scanline count:

  • For 60 Hz: Coders raised the vertical total to 525 lines (e.g., standard Mode X at 320x240 resolution), yielding: \[\frac{25,175,000}{800 \times 525} \approx 59.94\text{ Hz}\]
  • For 50 Hz: The vertical total was pushed even higher (around 625–628 lines), or the horizontal timings were stretched, slowing the refresh cycle down to precisely 50 Hz.

A 50 Hz refresh rate gave the CPU roughly 20 milliseconds per frame instead of the standard 14.2 milliseconds at 70 Hz. This extra execution window provided significantly more processor time to calculate complex 3D transforms, plasma routines, or audio synthesis before synchronizing to the next vertical blanking interrupt (in al, 0x3DA; test al, 8).
