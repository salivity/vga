Visual Dithering Artifacts in 16-Bit VGA

Downsampling a 24-bit true-color image to a 16-bit Video Graphics Array (VGA) display mode forces a reduction from 16.7 million colors to just 65,536 colors. Because 16-bit High Color typically allocates 5 bits for red, 6 bits for green, and 5 bits for blue (RGB 5:6:5), spatial dithering is often applied to blend neighboring pixels and simulate discarded color steps. While dithering prevents severe posterization and hard contour lines, it introduces several distinct visual artifacts across different regions of an image.

Structured Cross-Hatch and Checkerboard Noise

When ordered dithering (such as a Bayer matrix) is used, the image develops a rigid, repeating geometric texture. Because the algorithm applies a static threshold pattern across pixel coordinates, smooth gradients and flat color fields (like skies or skin tones) take on a visible screen-door or checkerboard appearance. This structured noise compromises the natural smoothness of continuous-tone imagery and becomes especially noticeable on modern high-resolution or magnified displays.

"Worm" Artifacts and Directional Smearing

Error-diffusion algorithms, like Floyd-Steinberg, redistribute quantization error to adjacent pixels that have not yet been rendered. In low-contrast gradients and near-white or near-black regions, this accumulation produces serpentine, line-like structures known as "worms." These directional streaks disrupt clean surfaces, causing uniform background areas to look contaminated by winding, fibrous textures aligned with the scanning direction of the algorithm.

Asymmetric Chromatic Noise

The standard 16-bit RGB 5:6:5 format provides 64 levels of green but only 32 levels each for red and blue. Because the red and blue channels experience twice the quantization error of the green channel, dithering noise is unevenly distributed across the color spectrum. This creates subtle magenta-green color fringing in neutral gray ramps, as the coarser red and blue dither patterns fight to balance against the finer green steps.

Texture Obliteration and High-Frequency Grain

In finely detailed textures—such as fabric weaves, distant foliage, or subtle facial pores—dithering introduces random or pseudo-random high-frequency speckling. The added dither noise competes with the image's native high-frequency information, effectively wiping out subtle micro-contrast and replacing authentic fine detail with artificial salt-and-pepper grain.

Edge Bleeding and Ghost Halos

In error-diffusion pipelines, high-contrast boundaries (such as a dark object against a bright background) generate large mathematical error values. When these large residuals are pushed across sharp edges into adjacent light or dark areas, they create "echoes" or halos that trail along the processing path. This leaves bright fringes next to dark silhouettes or dark smudges adjacent to sharp highlights.
