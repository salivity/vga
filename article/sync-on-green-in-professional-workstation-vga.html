Sync-on-Green in Professional Workstation VGA

Sync-on-green (SoG), often designated as RGsB, was a video signaling method widely utilized by high-end Unix workstations from manufacturers like Sun Microsystems, Silicon Graphics (SGI), Hewlett-Packard, and Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC). This article examines the technical implementation of sync-on-green in workstation-class video systems, detailing how horizontal and vertical timing signals were combined and multiplexed directly onto the green color channel, the electrical voltage standards governing the composite waveform, the role of specialized RAMDAC hardware, and how receiving displays recovered the timing data.

The Purpose of Sync-on-Green in Workstations

Standard consumer Video Graphics Array (VGA) interfaces typically rely on five distinct coaxial lines (RGBHV): red, green, blue, horizontal sync, and vertical sync. In contrast, early professional workstations prioritized high-bandwidth video, large CRT displays, and heavily shielded multi-coaxial cabling to maintain signal integrity at extreme resolutions (such as 1280×1024 or 1600×1200 at high refresh rates).

To reduce cable bulk and eliminate skew between discrete timing lines and color signals, workstation vendors opted for three-wire (RGsB) transmission. While workstations commonly featured proprietary connectors like 13W3 or direct BNC connectors, many also used standard DE-15 (VGA) ports wired to output sync-on-green, requiring the display to detect and strip sync pulses directly from the green analog channel.

Electrical Characteristics and Signal Structure

In a standard separate-sync RGB configuration, the video channels carry analog signals ranging from 0.0 volts (black level) to 0.7 volts (peak white) terminated into a 75-ohm impedance, with horizontal and vertical sync driven as separate TTL logic levels (0V to 5V).

Sync-on-green merges these signals by shifting the baseline reference:

  • Peak White: +0.7 V above the blanking level.
  • Black/Blanking Level: 0.0 V (the reference baseline for video data).
  • Sync Tip: -0.3 V below the blanking level.
  • Total Voltage Swing: 1.0 V peak-to-peak (\(1.0\text{ V}_{\text{p-p}}\)).

During the horizontal and vertical blanking intervals, the active video output drops to the blanking level (0.0 V). The sync generator then pulls the green line down to -0.3 V to generate synchronization pulses. Because the sync pulses exist entirely in the negative voltage region relative to black, the display's beam-intensity circuitry naturally ignores them as "blacker-than-black," preventing sync pulses from visibly distorting the raster scan.

Hardware Generation: The Role of the RAMDAC

Workstation graphics framebuffers (such as SGI's Newport and Impact architectures or Sun's TGX and Creator series) relied heavily on integrated Random-Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converters (RAMDACs) manufactured by companies like Brooktree (e.g., Bt458, Bt459, Bt478) and Inmos.

The implementation within these systems operated as follows:

  1. Composite Sync Generation: The graphics controller's timing engine combined separate horizontal sync (HSYNC) and vertical sync (VSYNC) into a single composite sync (CSYNC) line using digital logic (typically an XOR gate or dedicated state machine to handle vertical equalization pulses).
  2. RAMDAC Sync Control: The composite sync signal was fed into a dedicated SYNC input pin on the RAMDAC.
  3. Current-Steering DAC Output: The RAMDAC possessed internal 75-ohm current sources for all three color channels, but only the green output channel integrated a sync-current generator. When the SYNC pin was asserted during blanking intervals, an auxiliary internal current sink switched on, drawing the green output pin down by an additional 40% of its normal dynamic range (creating the -0.3 V pulse).
  4. Register-Level Activation: Hardware could dynamically enable or disable sync-on-green via internal control registers inside the RAMDAC. Setting the appropriate bit directed the chip to superimpose CSYNC onto the green output while disabling the discrete sync output pins.

Workstation Firmware and Configuration

In many workstation platforms, the video output mode was configured before the operating system loaded. For example, Sun workstations managed video configuration through the OpenBoot PROM (OBP). System administrators used NVRAM parameters or the setenv command to dictate monitor timings and sync behavior:

  • Firmware probed the monitor via DDC or vendor-specific sense pins (common on 13W3 connectors).
  • If a fixed-frequency workstation monitor was detected, the firmware initialized the graphics controller to output composite sync on green.
  • If adapted to a multi-sync PC monitor via an adapter cable, jumpers inside the adapter or specific monitor sense pins signaled the framebuffer to split sync into standard RGBHV via board-level multiplexers, provided the hardware supported it.

Display-Side Decoding

Monitors designed for workstation use contained specialized sync-stripper circuitry at their input stages. An analog comparator or a dedicated sync-separator IC (such as the industry-standard LM1881) monitored the green input line:

  1. DC Restoration/Clamping: The incoming AC-coupled video signal was clamped to establish a stable DC reference at the blanking level (0.0 V).
  2. Level Slicing: A comparator evaluated the signal against a threshold voltage set midway between blanking and the sync tip (typically around -0.15 V).
  3. Pulse Extraction: Any signal dropping below this threshold triggered the comparator, effectively reconstructing the digital CSYNC pulse train while routing the pure 0.0V–0.7V color information to the green electron gun amplifier.
