Pixel Aspect Ratio of 320x200 on a 4:3 VGA Screen

Displaying a 320x200 graphic on a standard 4:3 Video Graphics Array (VGA) monitor results in a Pixel Aspect Ratio (PAR) of 5:6, or approximately 0.833:1. This article explains the mathematical formula used to calculate this aspect ratio, details why vintage computer systems used non-square pixels, and addresses how this stretching affects visual rendering.

Mathematical Calculation of the Ratio

The relationship between the display shape, resolution, and pixel shape is governed by the following formula:

\[\text{Display Aspect Ratio (DAR)} = \text{Storage Aspect Ratio (SAR)} \times \text{Pixel Aspect Ratio (PAR)}\]

To determine the PAR:

  1. Identify the Display Aspect Ratio (DAR): Standard CRT VGA monitors have a physical screen ratio of 4:3 (approximately 1.333:1).
  2. Identify the Storage Aspect Ratio (SAR): The native resolution is 320 pixels wide by 200 pixels tall, which simplifies to a ratio of 16:10 (or 1.6:1).
  3. Solve for PAR: \[\text{PAR} = \frac{\text{DAR}}{\text{SAR}} = \frac{4/3}{16/10} = \frac{4}{3} \times \frac{10}{16} = \frac{40}{48} = \frac{5}{6}\]

Expressed as a decimal, 5/6 is approximately 0.8333. Alternatively, when expressed as width-to-height relative to a unit of 1, each pixel has a ratio of 1:1.2.

Visual Characteristics of 5:6 Pixels

Because the pixel aspect ratio is 5:6, the individual pixels are non-square—they are physically taller than they are wide. Each pixel is 20% taller than its width (\(1 \times 1.2\)).

When an image created with square pixels is mapped to a 320x200 buffer without correction and displayed on a 4:3 monitor, the image appears vertically stretched. To display circles and squares correctly on vintage hardware, artists had to account for this distortion by drawing shapes wider in the framebuffer so that the monitor's physical scan lines stretched them into proper geometry.

Why VGA Used 320x200

The 320x200 resolution became prominent with IBM's introduction of VGA Mode 13h in 1987. The 320x200 format required exactly 64,000 bytes of video memory, fitting neatly within the standard 64 KB memory bank limit of early PC architectures while allowing 256 colors per pixel.

VGA monitors expected a vertical scan frequency matching a 400-line or 480-line raster. To fill a 4:3 monitor vertically without requiring extra video RAM, the VGA controller used "double scanning," displaying each horizontal line of the 200-line image twice to create a 400-line raster. This scanline doubling, mapped across the 4:3 screen surface, physically established the 5:6 pixel aspect ratio.
