How Integer Scaling Preserves 320x200 VGA Games

Modern high-resolution displays often render retro 320x200 Video Graphics Array (VGA) games with unwanted blur and distortion due to standard interpolation algorithms. Integer scaling resolves this issue by magnifying each original pixel by a whole-number factor, ensuring that classic DOS games retain their intended razor-sharp, blocky aesthetic without introducing edge-softening artifacts.

The Problem with Conventional Upscaling

The standard resolution of early PC VGA gaming was 320x200 pixels. When a modern 1080p (1920x1080) or 4K (3840x2160) monitor receives this low-resolution signal, it cannot map the image directly without leaving the vast majority of the screen blank.

To fill the display, modern monitors and operating systems typically use bilinear or bicubic filtering. These algorithms calculate color averages between neighboring pixels to stretch the image smoothly across non-integer dimensions. While effective for photographs and modern 3D graphics, this averaging destroys retro pixel art. Sharp outlines become muddy, contrast is diminished, and fine in-game text becomes difficult to read.

The Mechanics of Integer Scaling

Integer scaling, also known as nearest-neighbor scaling by integer multiples, replaces fractional averaging with uniform multiplication. Instead of guessing intermediate color values, the display maps every single original pixel to a solid grid of identical sub-pixels.

For example, when displaying a 320x200 game on a 4K panel:

  • A 9x vertical multiplier and a 9x horizontal multiplier scale the image to 2880x1800.
  • Every single source pixel becomes a pristine 9x9 block of identical pixels on the screen.
  • Because the color values do not blend with neighboring blocks, the transitions between edges remain completely instantaneous and sharp.

Any remaining space around the scaled image is filled with black borders (pillarboxing or letterboxing), which prevents the distortion that occurs when forcing an image into an incompatible physical display boundary.

Addressing the 320x200 Aspect Ratio

One unique characteristic of the 320x200 VGA standard is its native 16:10 mathematical ratio, which was physically stretched to a 4:3 aspect ratio by cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors of the era. The pixels were rectangular, taller than they were wide, rather than square.

Integer scaling approaches this characteristic in two distinct ways:

  1. Square-Pixel Integer Scaling: Magnifies the original 320x200 image by equal horizontal and vertical integers (e.g., 5x5 or 9x9). This produces the sharpest possible output, though it presents the graphics wider than they originally appeared on a CRT.
  2. Aspect-Corrected Integer Scaling: Applies different integer multipliers to the axes or pairs an integer scale with a high-resolution nearest-neighbor stretch to approximate the CRT's 4:3 geometry. Because modern screens offer immense pixel density, minor adjustments to match the 4:3 target introduce virtually no visible blurring when anchored to an integer baseline.

By locking the magnification process to whole numbers, integer scaling completely eliminates the smearing caused by fractional math, allowing 320x200 VGA games to be viewed with pixel-perfect clarity on modern hardware.
