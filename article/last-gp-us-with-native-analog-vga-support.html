Last GPUs with Native Analog VGA Support

For decades, analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) signals were the universal standard for connecting PCs to computer monitors. As display resolutions grew and refresh rates accelerated, the industry transitioned to pure digital interfaces, permanently retiring native analog outputs from modern graphics cards. This article covers the final graphics card generations from Nvidia and AMD that included native analog video capability—specifically the Nvidia GeForce 900 series and the AMD Radeon 300 series—explaining when and why internal digital-to-analog converters were finally removed from consumer hardware.

Nvidia: GeForce 900 Series (Maxwell)

The Nvidia GeForce 900 series, powered by the second-generation Maxwell architecture, was the final generation of GeForce cards to support native analog VGA output. Flagship and mid-range desktop models—including the GeForce GTX 980, GTX 970, GTX 960, and GTX 950—routinely featured at least one DVI-I (Digital Visual Interface - Integrated) port.

Because DVI-I includes dedicated physical pins for analog RGB and sync signals driven by an integrated RAMDAC (Random-Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converter), users could attach simple, passive DVI-to-VGA adapters to connect legacy VGA monitors.

Starting with the GeForce 10 series (Pascal architecture, such as the GTX 1080 and GTX 1060), Nvidia completely eliminated the internal RAMDAC and transitioned all DVI ports to DVI-D (Digital only). As a result, Pascal and all subsequent architectures require active digital-to-analog converters to output a signal to an analog VGA monitor.

AMD: Radeon R7 / R9 300 Series

The AMD Radeon R7 and R9 300 series was the last family from AMD to include models with native analog VGA capabilities. Many cards in this lineup (such as the R7 360, R7 370, and select partner designs) retained DVI-I ports with native analog pins, as well as native VGA (D-Sub) connectors on entry-level models.

AMD began phasing out native analog signaling earlier than Nvidia on higher-end silicon. The Hawaii GPU core (seen on the R9 290, R9 290X, and later the R9 390 and 390X) as well as the Fiji architecture (R9 Fury series) lacked integrated analog display controllers, offering only pure digital outputs like DVI-D, HDMI, and DisplayPort. However, several lower-tier 300-series models built on refined earlier architectures still retained native analog pinouts.

The definitive end of native analog support across AMD’s entire portfolio occurred with the launch of the Radeon RX 400 series (Polaris architecture). Beginning with the RX 480, RX 470, and RX 460, AMD permanently omitted internal display DACs and analog pins from all consumer graphics hardware.

Why Native VGA Was Abandoned

Modern display technologies rendered analog video obsolete for several reasons:

  • Die Space and Architecture: Removing the internal RAMDAC freed up silicon real estate on the GPU die, reducing heat and manufacturing complexity.
  • Bandwidth Limitations: Analog VGA struggles to maintain image clarity and signal integrity at resolutions beyond 1080p, and it cannot reliably support the high refresh rates (120Hz and higher) standard in modern gaming monitors.
  • Modern Standards: DisplayPort and HDMI carry high-bandwidth digital video, multi-channel audio, and copy protection (HDCP) over a single cable, fully replacing the functions of legacy analog connectors.
