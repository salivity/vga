How VGA Horizontal Blanking Works on CRT Monitors

This article explains how Video Graphics Array (VGA) horizontal blanking timing controls the electron beam inside a Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) display during horizontal retrace. It details the structural phases of the horizontal line cycle—including the active display time, front porch, horizontal sync pulse, and back porch—and demonstrates how electrical voltage levels are manipulated to suppress beam emission, preventing unwanted retrace lines from distorting the rendered image.

In a CRT monitor, an image is formed by an electron gun firing a focused beam of electrons across a phosphor-coated screen. Magnetic deflection coils sweep this beam horizontally from left to right to draw a single scanline. Once the beam reaches the right edge of the visible display area, it must return to the left side to begin the next scanline. This transit back to the left is called horizontal retrace. If the electron gun continues emitting electrons during retrace, bright diagonal streaks—known as retrace artifacts or ghost lines—appear across the screen.

VGA solves this issue by establishing a precise horizontal blanking interval (HBI) governed by pixel clock timing. The HBI is divided into three distinct phases:

  1. Front Porch: Immediately following the active pixel data of a scanline, the front porch acts as a short temporal buffer. During this phase, the graphics card drives the red, green, and blue (RGB) analog lines down to their blanking level (0.0 volts). This guarantees that the electron gun is fully extinguished before the physical deflection coils attempt to reverse the beam's direction.

  2. Horizontal Sync (HSync): Once the front porch concludes, the graphics controller pulls the HSync signal to its active state (either low or high, depending on the VGA mode). This pulse commands the monitor's horizontal sweep oscillator and flyback transformer to rapidly reverse the magnetic field in the deflection yoke. The sudden change in the magnetic field causes the electron beam to physically snap back to the left side of the tube.

  3. Back Porch: After the HSync pulse ends, the beam has returned to the left margin, but the deflection circuitry requires time to settle and stabilize its sweep rate. The back porch provides this stabilization window. The RGB signals remain held at 0.0 volts throughout this period, keeping the electron gun off until the beam is aligned and moving at a constant speed, ready for the next line's active video.

The physical extinguishing of the beam is governed by voltage thresholds. In the standard VGA specification, active video signals range from 0.0V (black) to 0.7V (maximum brightness). When the video controller enters any part of the horizontal blanking period, the digital-to-analog converter (DAC) outputs 0.0V across all three color channels. At 0.0V, the control grid voltage relative to the cathode in the CRT drops below the visual cut-off threshold.

This negative grid bias prevents electrons from escaping the cathode. Without an electron stream flowing through the tube, no phosphors are excited while the deflection coils pull the beam path back to the left side of the display. Once the back porch expires and the beam reaches the precise starting coordinate of the next visible scanline, the DAC begins outputting active pixel voltages again, cleanly drawing the new line without visible artifacts.
