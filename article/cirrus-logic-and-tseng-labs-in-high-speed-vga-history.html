Cirrus Logic and Tseng Labs in High-Speed VGA History

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, the personal computer industry shifted from basic text displays to rich graphical user interfaces and fast-paced games. This transition demanded graphics hardware that could surpass the limitations of IBM's original Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard. This article explores how two rival semiconductor companies, Tseng Labs and Cirrus Logic, engineered high-speed VGA and Super VGA (SVGA) chipsets that revolutionized memory bandwidth, pioneered hardware acceleration, and established high-performance PC graphics.

The VGA Bottleneck and the Rise of Super VGA

When IBM introduced the VGA standard in 1987, it supported a baseline resolution of 640x480 with 16 colors or 320x200 with 256 colors. While revolutionary at the time, the original architecture was severely bottlenecked by the slow 8-bit and 16-bit Industry Standard Architecture (ISA) bus. Furthermore, standard VGA cards relied entirely on the host CPU to manipulate video memory. As software demanded higher resolutions, 24-bit true color, and faster refresh rates, third-party silicon designers stepped in to eliminate these performance constraints.

Tseng Labs: Raw Bandwidth and DOS Gaming Dominance

Tseng Labs established itself as the benchmark for raw memory throughput and speed. The company's breakthrough came with the ET4000 family, introduced in late 1989. While earlier chips like the ET3000 laid the groundwork, the ET4000 became legendary among PC enthusiasts and original equipment manufacturers (OEMs).

Tseng Labs achieved superior performance through an innovative memory controller architecture. The ET4000 utilized memory interleaving and optimized internal pipelines that minimized wait states when the CPU accessed video RAM. This gave Tseng Labs graphics boards unmatched frame rates in frame-buffer-intensive DOS environments. Classic 3D and pseudo-3D titles, including Wolfenstein 3D and Doom, ran significantly faster on ET4000-based cards than on competing hardware.

As bus standards evolved, Tseng Labs adapted the ET4000 line to the VESA Local Bus (VLB) and PCI architectures. With the later ET4000/W32 series, Tseng Labs introduced dedicated hardware acceleration for graphic user interface (GUI) operations, maintaining its reputation for speed until the market shifted decisively toward dedicated 3D acceleration.

Cirrus Logic: Integration and Affordable GUI Acceleration

While Tseng Labs targeted peak throughput, Cirrus Logic revolutionized the market through high-level silicon integration and cost-effective acceleration. In the early 1990s, a standard graphics card required separate chips for the controller, the RAMDAC (which converts digital image data into analog display signals), and the clock generator.

Cirrus Logic changed this with the CL-GD542x series, notably the GD5422, GD5426, and GD5428 chipsets. Cirrus Logic was among the first to integrate the controller, RAMDAC, and clock synthesizer into a single, low-cost chip. This drastically reduced manufacturing costs, motherboard real estate, and power consumption, making high-speed SVGA accessible to the mainstream desktop and notebook markets.

Beyond cost efficiency, Cirrus Logic focused heavily on the emerging Microsoft Windows environment. Starting with the CL-GD5426, the company integrated an internal 32-bit BitBLT (Bit Block Transfer) engine. This hardware engine offloaded routine GUI operations—such as window dragging, line drawing, pattern fills, and hardware cursor management—from the host CPU directly onto the graphics chipset. This acceleration transformed Windows from a sluggish graphical shell into a responsive working environment.

Lasting Impact on the PC Graphics Industry

Together, Tseng Labs and Cirrus Logic drove the development of the VESA (Video Electronics Standards Association) standards, ensuring cross-compatibility for high resolutions and color depths across diverse software. Tseng Labs pushed the boundaries of raw bus efficiency and memory bandwidth, while Cirrus Logic set the standard for high integration, lower system costs, and specialized 2D acceleration. Their fierce competition dismantled IBM's proprietary control over PC display standards and laid the architectural foundation for the dedicated 2D and 3D graphics processors that followed.
