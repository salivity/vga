VGA to Composite Sync: How Sync Separation Works

Connecting a standard Video Graphics Array (VGA) source to a monitor requiring composite sync involves converting separate timing channels into a single unified stream, which the display must then interpret. This article explains the technical process of adapting VGA's separate horizontal and vertical sync signals for a composite sync display, detailing how sync combining occurs at the interface and how internal sync separation circuitry decodes the pulses to construct a stable picture.

Sync Format Differences

Standard VGA outputs video in the RGBHV format. In this arrangement, Red, Green, and Blue color data are transmitted alongside two dedicated, independent timing lines: Horizontal Sync (H-Sync) and Vertical Sync (V-Sync). Both signals typically operate at Transistor-Transistor Logic (TTL) voltage levels (around 5V peak-to-peak).

In contrast, displays requiring Composite Sync (RGBS or CSync)—common in arcade monitors, broadcast equipment, and specialized CRT displays—expect all timing information delivered over a single conductor.

Sync Combining: Preparing the VGA Signal

Because VGA provides two separate sync outputs, they must first be combined before reaching a composite sync input. This is typically achieved using an active logic circuit, such as an Exclusive OR (XOR) gate:

  1. Pulse Preservation: An XOR gate combines the high-frequency H-Sync pulses (which control line-by-line drawing) and the low-frequency V-Sync pulses (which reset the beam to the top of the screen).
  2. Inversion Handling: An XOR circuit properly handles sync polarities. Whether the VGA source outputs positive or negative sync pulses, the gate ensures the combined waveform retains correct pulse widths and timing edges without phase cancellation.

Once combined, this single CSync stream travels to the display alongside the RGB video channels.

The Role of Sync Separation in the Display

Once the combined signal enters the display, the monitor's deflection circuits cannot directly use the multiplexed line. The display requires separate control voltages to drive its horizontal sweep (line deflection) and vertical sweep (field deflection). An internal sync separator—implemented via dedicated ICs (such as the LM1881) or discrete analog filtering—splits the composite stream back into independent triggers.

Sync separation relies on the significant difference in duration between horizontal and vertical pulses:

  • Horizontal Sync Extraction (High-Pass / Differentiating): Horizontal pulses occur rapidly (e.g., 31.5 kHz for standard VGA or 15.7 kHz for standard-definition CRT) and have a very short duration, usually around a few microseconds. The sync separator uses a differentiator network (high-pass filter) to detect the sharp, leading edges of these pulses, generating immediate timing spikes to trigger the horizontal sweep oscillator.
  • Vertical Sync Extraction (Low-Pass / Integrating): Vertical sync pulses last much longer than horizontal pulses. The separator routes the composite signal through an integrator network (low-pass filter). Because standard horizontal pulses are too short to charge the circuit's capacitor significantly, they are ignored. When the longer vertical sync pulse arrives, the capacitor accumulates enough charge to cross a voltage threshold, firing the vertical reset trigger.

Maintaining Lock with Serration Pulses

A critical challenge during sync separation is keeping the display's horizontal oscillator synchronized while the vertical pulse is active. If the horizontal pulses stopped during the vertical blanking interval, the horizontal deflection would lose timing lock, causing image distortion or tearing at the top of the screen.

To solve this, composite sync uses serration pulses and equalization pulses. These are narrow slots cut directly into the broad vertical sync block. They allow the integrator to remain charged for vertical sync detection while simultaneously providing continuous falling edges to keep the horizontal differentiator engaged throughout the entire vertical refresh cycle.
