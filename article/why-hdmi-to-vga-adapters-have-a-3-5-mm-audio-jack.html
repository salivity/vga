Why HDMI to VGA Adapters Have a 3.5mm Audio Jack

HDMI to Video Graphics Array (VGA) adapters often include a separate 3.5mm audio jack because HDMI transmits both video and audio signals, whereas VGA only transmits video. When an HDMI source is converted to a VGA display, the audio stream is naturally left behind. The integrated 3.5mm auxiliary port acts as an audio extractor, allowing users to route the sound to external speakers, headphones, or sound systems rather than losing it entirely during the video conversion process.

The fundamental reason for the audio jack lies in the technological differences between the two formats:

  • HDMI (High-Definition Multimedia Interface): A modern digital standard designed to carry high-definition video along with multi-channel digital audio through a single cable.
  • VGA (Video Graphics Array): An analog standard introduced in 1987 designed purely for video display signals. It possesses no pins or wiring dedicated to carrying audio.

When modern devices such as laptops, game consoles, or media players output through an HDMI port, they bundle video and sound together. If a user connects one of these devices to an older VGA monitor or projector, the receiving display has no native way to process or play the incoming audio.

To resolve this issue, HDMI to VGA adapters contain an internal active digital-to-analog converter (DAC) chip. As the digital signal enters the adapter, the converter splits the stream. It translates the digital video data into an analog RGB signal for the VGA output, while simultaneously extracting the digital audio data and converting it into standard analog stereo sound.

This decoded sound is sent directly to the 3.5mm audio output jack. From there, users can plug in an auxiliary cable to send the audio to desktop speakers, headphones, or an external amplifier. Without this dedicated audio port, the audio portion of the HDMI transmission would simply be lost, leaving the user with video on their VGA display but no sound.
