Why CRT Monitors Needed 75 Hz or 85 Hz Refresh Rates

During the era of cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors and Video Graphics Array (VGA) standards, running a display at a 75 Hz or 85 Hz refresh rate was considered essential for daily computing. Unlike modern flat panels, CRTs used scanning electron beams that caused pixels to rapidly decay in brightness, creating visible flicker at lower frequencies. Setting a CRT to 75 Hz or 85 Hz eliminated noticeable flicker, complied with emerging workplace ergonomics standards, prevented visual fatigue, and balanced the technical limits of VGA hardware.

The Mechanism of CRT Phosphor Decay

To understand the necessity of higher refresh rates, one must understand how a CRT produces an image. A CRT monitor does not hold an image statically like a modern liquid-crystal display (LCD). Instead, an electron gun fires a focused beam of electrons across the back of a glass screen coated with phosphorescent material.

The beam scans the screen line by line, from top to bottom. As the beam strikes the phosphors, they glow brightly and immediately begin to decay. By the time the electron gun finishes drawing the bottom of the screen, the phosphors at the top are already dimming. Because of this continuous decay, the screen must be constantly redrawn to maintain the illusion of a steady image.

The Problem with 60 Hz: The Flicker Threshold

Early personal computers commonly defaulted to a 60 Hz refresh rate, drawing the full screen 60 times per second. While 60 Hz was sufficient for older television broadcasts due to interlacing and long-persistence phosphors, it was problematic for computer monitors for several reasons:

  • Short-Persistence Phosphors: Computer CRTs required fast-decaying phosphors to prevent ghosting or smearing when moving a cursor or scrolling text. Because the light faded so quickly, the dip in luminance between scans was pronounced.
  • Human Critical Flicker Frequency (CFF): The human visual system can perceive light oscillations up to roughly 60 to 70 Hz, especially in peripheral vision where the retina is more sensitive to motion and rapid changes in light. At 60 Hz, users sitting close to a bright white document or spreadsheet would actively perceive the screen pulsing.
  • Fluorescent Lighting Interference: Most office environments used fluorescent lighting, which typically oscillated at 50 Hz or 60 Hz depending on the local AC power grid. A monitor running at 60 Hz under 60 Hz lighting created a beat frequency—an optical strobing effect that significantly intensified the perception of flicker.

These factors caused rapid eye strain, severe headaches, visual fatigue, and degraded productivity during extended computer use.

Why 75 Hz Became the Baseline

To combat ergonomic issues in offices, standards organizations began evaluating monitor refresh rates in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) recognized that pushing the refresh rate to 75 Hz raised the redraw speed safely above the critical flicker frequency of the majority of the population.

At 75 Hz, each frame refreshed every 13.3 milliseconds (compared to 16.6 milliseconds at 60 Hz). This reduced the decay interval enough that the human eye perceived the phosphors as continuously illuminated, providing a baseline level of comfort for general office work.

Why 85 Hz Became the Gold Standard

While 75 Hz resolved flicker for most users, a subset of the population with higher flicker sensitivity could still perceive instability, particularly when viewing bright white backgrounds at larger screen sizes (such as 17-inch and 19-inch CRTs).

European regulatory bodies, led by the Swedish Confederation of Professional Employees (TCO), introduced strict ergonomics mandates including TCO '92 and TCO '95. These standards established that a CRT monitor must support at least 85 Hz at its standard resolution to be certified flicker-free.

At 85 Hz:

  • The refresh interval dropped to roughly 11.7 milliseconds.
  • The screen refreshed fast enough that virtually 100% of human users perceived a completely stable, static image.
  • Interference with ambient 50/60 Hz artificial lighting was neutralized.

The Bandwidth Sweet Spot

Monitors and graphics cards could theoretically run at even higher rates, such as 100 Hz or 120 Hz, but 85 Hz remained the standard recommendation due to hardware constraints.

A CRT's performance was dictated by the bandwidth of the VGA cable, the graphics card’s RAMDAC (random-access memory digital-to-analog converter), and the monitor's horizontal scan rate. Pushing a monitor beyond 85 Hz at popular resolutions like 1024x768 or 1280x1024 often exceeded the hardware’s bandwidth limit, resulting in blurry text, reduced color clarity, or the inability to display the image altogether.

Operating between 75 Hz and 85 Hz provided the ideal equilibrium: it fully eliminated visible phosphor flicker to protect users' eyesight while staying within the analog bandwidth limits of standard VGA hardware.
