Outputting 15 kHz and 31 kHz Video from One FPGA Port

Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) can easily generate both 15 kHz arcade video and 31 kHz Video Graphics Array (VGA) video from the exact same physical connector. Because an FPGA provides fully customizable digital logic, it can alter video timing registers, sync delivery methods, and pixel clock frequencies on the fly. By pairing the FPGA’s internal timing generators with an appropriate digital-to-analog converter (DAC) and a standard connector—most commonly a standard DE-15 port—a single piece of hardware can drive both authentic low-frequency arcade monitors and standard PC VGA displays.

Understanding the Timing Differences

The primary distinction between arcade video and VGA video is the horizontal scan rate:

  • 15 kHz Video: Standard-resolution arcade monitors and standard-definition CRT televisions typically utilize a horizontal frequency of approximately 15.7 kHz. This produces classic 240p or 480i video signals with vertical refresh rates around 60 Hz (NTSC) or 50 Hz (PAL).
  • 31 kHz Video: Standard VGA runs at roughly 31.5 kHz horizontal frequency to produce a 640x480 progressive image (480p) at 60 Hz.

An FPGA handles both modes by switching the internal clock dividers that drive the video state machine. By alternating between different counter thresholds for horizontal display, front porch, sync pulse, and back porch timings, the FPGA shifts between 15 kHz and 31 kHz operation instantly.

Synchronization Configuration

A physical connector must route the synchronization signals appropriately, as 15 kHz and 31 kHz formats typically utilize different sync standards:

  1. VGA (31 kHz): Standard VGA expects separate horizontal sync (HSync) and vertical sync (VSync) lines, known as RGBHV.
  2. Arcade (15 kHz): Most arcade monitors, JAMMA harnesses, and broadcast video monitors (PVMs) require a single Composite Sync (CSync) signal, known as RGBS.

Because FPGA pins can be routed dynamically, the hardware can output separate HSync and VSync pulses for 31 kHz VGA mode across two distinct pins. When 15 kHz mode is required, the internal FPGA logic can combine HSync and VSync using an XOR or NOR logic operation into a single CSync signal and route it directly over the original HSync pin (Pin 13 on a standard DE-15 connector). The monitor receiving the 15 kHz signal reads Pin 13 as CSync, leaving Pin 14 (VSync) unused or tied low.

The Physical Interface: DE-15 Port

The standard DE-15 (commonly referred to as a VGA port) is the most widely adopted physical connector for this dual-mode application. It features 15 pins that support all required signals:

  • Pins 1, 2, and 3: Red, Green, and Blue analog lines.
  • Pins 6, 7, and 8: Ground returns for the respective color lines.
  • Pin 13: Horizontal Sync for 31 kHz VGA, or Composite Sync for 15 kHz arcade video.
  • Pin 14: Vertical Sync for 31 kHz VGA (unconnected in 15 kHz RGBS mode).

By driving these pins through a video DAC (such as an ADV7125 or a resistor ladder network), the FPGA outputs clean analog color voltages alongside the corresponding sync mode.

Voltage Levels and Electrical Considerations

While an FPGA can output both frequencies through the same connector, hardware designers must account for electrical level differences between consumer monitors and true arcade chassis:

  • Standard VGA Levels: VGA specifications mandate 0.7 V peak-to-peak (p-p) for color lines terminated at 75 ohms, with TTL-level sync (3.3V to 5V).
  • Arcade Chassis Levels: Traditional arcade CRT chassis often require amplified video signals ranging from 2.0 V p-p up to 5.0 V p-p for colors, and 5V TTL for sync.

When connecting an FPGA directly to an original arcade chassis, an external video amplifier or a specialized interface board (such as a modern JAMMA adapter) is frequently used to step up the 0.7 V p-p DAC output to native arcade levels. However, if the target is a professional video monitor (PVM/BVM) or a multi-sync CRT that accepts 15 kHz at 0.7 V p-p, the FPGA's native VGA port output can be wired directly using a passive DE-15-to-BNC or SCART breakout cable.

Practical Implementation

This dual-frequency capability is common in modern retro-computing and arcade preservation platforms like the MiSTer FPGA project. The platform's I/O board uses a single DE-15 output to drive 31 kHz PC monitors, 15 kHz standard-definition CRTs, and even component video (YPbPr) simply by modifying the FPGA's software configuration file to change the pixel clock and sync routing.
