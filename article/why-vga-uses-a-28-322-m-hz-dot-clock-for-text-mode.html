Why VGA Uses a 28.322 MHz Dot Clock for Text Mode

When IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) in 1987, it established two primary hardware pixel clocks: 25.175 MHz for 640-pixel graphic modes and 28.322 MHz for 720-pixel text modes. The selection of the 28.322 MHz dot clock was the mathematical consequence of preserving high-readability 9-pixel-wide text characters while matching the display monitor’s fixed 31.46875 kHz horizontal scanning frequency at a 70 Hz vertical refresh rate.

To understand the clock frequency, one must look at the display requirements IBM carried over from the earlier Monochrome Display Adapter (MDA). MDA text was praised for its crispness because it used character cells that were 9 pixels wide by 14 scanlines tall. Over an 80-column display, this produced an active horizontal resolution of 720 pixels (80 columns × 9 pixels). When designing VGA, IBM aimed to improve upon this standard by implementing a 9×16 character box across 400 active scanlines, creating a 720×400 text resolution.

CRT monitors of the era required strict timing signals to steer the electron beam across the screen. VGA standardized on a horizontal line frequency of approximately 31.46875 kHz (often referred to as 31.5 kHz, which was double the traditional NTSC television line rate). For text display, the vertical refresh rate was set to 70.087 Hz across a frame composed of 449 total vertical lines, including the active display lines, front porch, vertical sync pulse, and back porch:

\[\text{Horizontal Frequency} = 70.087\text{ Hz} \times 449\text{ lines} = 31,468.75\text{ Hz}\]

For the horizontal beam to return to the left side of the screen safely, an analog monitor also requires horizontal blanking intervals (front porch, sync, and back porch). IBM structured the text mode timing around a total horizontal frame of 100 character clocks per line.

Because each character was 9 dots wide, every horizontal scanline required 900 total pixel dots:

\[\text{Total Dots per Line} = 100\text{ characters} \times 9\text{ pixels} = 900\text{ dots}\]

Of these 900 dots, 720 dots (80 characters) were visible, and the remaining 180 dots (20 characters) served as the horizontal blanking and sync interval.

Multiplying the total horizontal dots per line by the required horizontal line frequency determines the exact pixel clock required to paint those pixels:

\[\text{Pixel Clock} = 900\text{ dots} \times 31,468.75\text{ lines per second} = 28,321,875\text{ Hz}\]

This calculated value of 28.321875 MHz is the precise origin of the rounded 28.322 MHz oscillator.

By placing both a 25.175 MHz crystal oscillator (which generated 800 total dots at 8 pixels per character for 640-pixel graphics) and a 28.322 MHz crystal oscillator on the original VGA adapter, IBM enabled the hardware to switch between graphics and 9-dot-wide text modes without changing the monitor's horizontal sweep rate. The electron beam scanned across the screen at the exact same speed in both modes, ensuring stable, flicker-free display transitions.
