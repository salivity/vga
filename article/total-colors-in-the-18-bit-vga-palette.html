Total Colors in the 18-Bit VGA Palette

The Video Graphics Array (VGA) display standard introduced by IBM in 1987 revolutionized PC visuals by implementing an 18-bit color palette. This article details the mathematical calculation behind the total number of colors that an 18-bit VGA system can generate, how the color channels are allocated, and the distinction between the total color space and the number of colors displayed on screen at one time.

An 18-bit Video Graphics Array palette can produce a total of 262,144 potential colors.

This total is derived from binary mathematics: raising 2 to the power of 18 (\(2^{18}\)) results in 262,144 unique color combinations. In the standard VGA hardware implementation, this color depth is achieved using a digital-to-analog converter (RAMDAC) that divides the 18 bits evenly across the three primary additive color channels: red, green, and blue (RGB).

Each color channel is allocated 6 bits of data:

  • Red: 6 bits (\(2^6 = 64\) shades)
  • Green: 6 bits (\(2^6 = 64\) shades)
  • Blue: 6 bits (\(2^6 = 64\) shades)

By multiplying the individual intensity levels of each channel (\(64 \times 64 \times 64\)), the hardware generates the complete gamut of 262,144 possible hues, ranging from solid black to pure white and every shade in between.

While the master palette consists of 262,144 potential colors, standard VGA hardware could not display all of these colors simultaneously due to memory constraints of the era. Instead, the system relied on an indexed palette. In standard modes, such as the popular Mode 13h (320x200 resolution), the hardware could display 256 colors at once, or 16 colors in higher resolutions like 640x480. Each of these active on-screen colors was mapped to an entry in a Color Lookup Table (CLUT), where any slot could be assigned any of the 262,144 shades available from the 18-bit space.
