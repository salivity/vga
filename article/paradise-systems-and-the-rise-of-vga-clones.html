Paradise Systems and the Rise of VGA Clones

When IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard in 1987 alongside its PS/2 computer line, it threatened to lock third-party graphics manufacturers out of the market. Paradise Systems countered this shift by reverse-engineering the technology and releasing some of the earliest, most reliable VGA-compatible silicon. This article explores how Paradise Systems, through chips like the PVGA1A and the Western Digital WD90C series, democratized the VGA standard, fueled an explosion of affordable third-party add-in boards, and laid the groundwork for the Super VGA era.

In April 1987, IBM integrated VGA directly onto the motherboards of its PS/2 computers, leaving traditional ISA-based graphics card manufacturers in jeopardy. Replicating VGA was significantly harder than cloning previous standards like CGA or EGA because IBM implemented VGA using proprietary gate arrays with undocumented registers. Paradise Systems was among the first companies to successfully achieve register-level hardware compatibility with IBM VGA, bypassing the need for software emulation layers that degraded performance.

The debut of the Paradise PVGA1A chipset in early 1988 transformed the PC clone ecosystem. Paradise did not just sell finished graphics cards; it provided silicon and reference designs to original equipment manufacturers (OEMs). Small- and mid-sized hardware vendors that lacked the research and development budgets to reverse-engineer IBM’s hardware could suddenly produce fully functional VGA cards for standard ISA bus slots. This shattered IBM’s exclusive hold on the technology and created an intensely competitive add-in card marketplace.

Compatibility was the cornerstone of the Paradise chipset's dominance. The PVGA1A, followed closely by the PVGA1B, provided native backwards compatibility with older display modes, including EGA, CGA, MDA, and Hercules graphics. Crucially, the Paradise hardware included autosensing capabilities that automatically matched the display mode to the software without requiring users to flip manual DIP switches on the back of the card. This reliability made Paradise-based cards the default safe choice for system builders and corporate buyers transitioning from legacy systems.

Shortly after its initial VGA success, Paradise Systems was acquired by Western Digital, which poured additional resources into refining the architecture. The resulting WD90C series (including the WD90C00, WD90C11, and WD90C31) brought substantial speed improvements and expanded memory support. These chips enabled resolutions beyond standard IBM VGA—pushing to 800x600 and 1024x768 resolutions with expanded color palettes—which directly contributed to the emergence of the Super VGA (SVGA) industry standard.

By lowering manufacturing barriers and outperforming IBM's original design, the Paradise Systems chipset family ensured that VGA became an open industry standard rather than a proprietary IBM feature. Their silicon powered millions of clone graphics cards worldwide, accelerating the PC industry's transition to higher-resolution color computing throughout the late 1980s and early 1990s.
