How 70 Hz VGA Text Mode Eliminated CRT Monitor Flicker

When IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) standard in 1987, it established a 70 Hz vertical refresh rate for its default 720x400 text mode, departing from the previous 60 Hz standard used by CGA and EGA. This change drastically reduced perceived screen flicker for office workers by pushing the display refresh rate beyond the human visual system's critical flicker frequency, combating phosphor decay rates, and eliminating visual interference caused by typical office fluorescent lighting.

The Problem with 60 Hz on Cathode Ray Tubes

Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitors create images by sweeping an electron beam across a phosphor-coated glass surface line by line. The phosphors emit light upon impact and begin to decay immediately afterward. For an image to appear solid, the beam must return and re-excite the phosphors before the eye registers the drop in luminance.

Earlier standards like CGA and EGA operated at a 60 Hz vertical refresh rate. While 60 Hz was often sufficient for lower-brightness television screens, it was inadequate for desktop computer monitors displaying high-contrast, static text. The phosphors used in typical computer monitors (often fast-decay P22 phosphors) lost their brightness rapidly between sweeps. This rapid cycle of illumination and decay created a pulsating effect that caused severe eyestrain, fatigue, and headaches during extended computing sessions.

Critical Flicker Fusion and Human Biology

The human visual system interprets flashing light as continuous illumination based on the Critical Flicker Fusion (CFF) threshold. For most individuals viewing a bright display, the CFF sits between 50 Hz and 60 Hz under direct central (foveal) vision.

However, peripheral vision is far more sensitive to motion and rapid changes in light. Office workers glancing around their desks, looking down at paperwork, or shifting their gaze across a 12-to-14-inch monitor regularly caught the screen in their peripheral field of view, where the CFF threshold can exceed 65 Hz. At 60 Hz, the display noticeably pulsed in their periphery.

By raising the refresh rate to 70 Hz (specifically ~70.087 Hz), the VGA standard moved the entire refresh cycle safely above the human eye's peripheral flicker threshold. The electron beam re-illuminated the screen every 14.2 milliseconds—instead of every 16.6 milliseconds at 60 Hz—meaning the phosphors never dropped to a luminance level where the human eye could discern the decay.

Eliminating Fluorescent Light Interference

The 70 Hz refresh rate also addressed an environmental issue common to corporate settings: fluorescent lighting. Standard magnetic ballasts in office fluorescent lights cycled at twice the AC power line frequency, producing a subtle flicker at 100 Hz (in 50 Hz regions) or 120 Hz (in 60 Hz regions).

A monitor running at 60 Hz under 120 Hz lighting generated visual beat frequencies. The minor phase shifts between the monitor's beam sweeps and the ambient room lighting created secondary, low-frequency strobe artifacts in the user's field of vision. Moving the display rate to 70 Hz broke this harmonic resonance with the power grid, eliminating the secondary flicker patterns that plagued 60 Hz displays.

The Mechanics of 720x400 at 70 Hz

IBM achieved this visual improvement without requiring overly expensive high-frequency monitor components by balancing the horizontal scan rate and pixel clock:

  • Pixel Clock: IBM incorporated a 28.322 MHz crystal oscillator on the VGA board specifically for text mode.
  • Horizontal Scan Rate: By combining 900 total horizontal pixel clocks per line (720 active pixels plus blanking intervals) with the 28.322 MHz clock, the monitor achieved a horizontal scan frequency of roughly 31.47 kHz.
  • Vertical Lines: The 720x400 text mode allocated 400 active scanlines, but with vertical blanking and front/back porches included, the frame comprised 449 total vertical lines.

Dividing the 31.46875 kHz horizontal line rate by 449 total lines yielded an exact vertical refresh rate of 70.086 Hz. This allowed IBM to render text characters in a sharp 9x16 pixel matrix—producing vastly superior character legibility compared to the 8x8 or 8x14 cells of earlier standards—while ensuring the entire image remained completely stable and comfortable to read throughout the workday.
