What Year Was IBM VGA Introduced?

IBM introduced the Video Graphics Array (VGA) display standard in 1987 alongside the launch of its Personal System/2 (PS/2) computer line. VGA marked a major leap forward in personal computer display technology, establishing a 640×480 pixel resolution baseline and popularizing 256-color modes. This article details the release year, key technical specifications, and the lasting influence the VGA standard had on personal computing hardware and gaming.

The 1987 Debut of VGA

In April 1987, International Business Machines (IBM) announced the PS/2 series of microcomputers. As an integrated feature on the PS/2 motherboards rather than an add-in card, IBM debuted the Video Graphics Array. It was designed to succeed earlier display systems like the Color Graphics Adapter (CGA) and Enhanced Graphics Adapter (EGA), standardizing higher graphical fidelity across desktop systems.

Key Technical Specifications

VGA introduced several improvements over the existing standards of the mid-1980s:

  • Resolution and Color Modes: The most famous modes included 640×480 pixels with 16 colors and 320×200 pixels with 256 colors chosen from an 18-bit palette (262,144 possible colors).
  • Analog Signaling: Unlike earlier digital RGB systems like EGA and CGA, VGA utilized analog video signals. This shift allowed smooth color gradation and virtually infinite potential color shades with appropriate digital-to-analog converters (RAMDAC).
  • Backward Compatibility: VGA maintained register-level compatibility with EGA, CGA, and MDA (Monochrome Display Adapter), ensuring legacy software continued to run without modification.

The 15-Pin D-Sub Connector

Along with the display chipset, IBM introduced the 15-pin D-subminiature connector, commonly known as the DE-15 or VGA port. This physical connector became the default display interface on monitors, graphics cards, projectors, and laptops for well over two decades before digital connections such as DVI, HDMI, and DisplayPort superseded it.

Industry Impact and Legacy

The release of VGA in 1987 reshaped PC gaming and visual computing throughout the late 1980s and the 1990s. The 320×200 256-color mode (Mode 13h) became the foundation for classic MS-DOS games such as Doom, Wolfenstein 3D, and LucasArts adventure titles. Third-party chip manufacturers quickly reverse-engineered and expanded upon the standard, developing Super VGA (SVGA) to deliver even higher resolutions and color depths, cementing 1987 as a pivotal milestone in graphical history.
