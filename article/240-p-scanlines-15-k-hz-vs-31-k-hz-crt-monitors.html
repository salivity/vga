240p Scanlines: 15 kHz vs 31 kHz CRT Monitors

Understanding how a 240p video signal behaves across different cathode ray tube (CRT) displays is essential for retro gaming enthusiasts and display purists. This article examines the physical and electrical differences in scanline structure when a 240p signal is displayed on a standard 15 kHz display versus a 31 kHz Video Graphics Array (VGA) monitor, detailing why the visual outputs vary dramatically in beam spacing, line blanking, and sync compatibility.

The Mechanics of a 240p Signal

A true 240p signal is a non-standard progressive video mode derived from standard-definition broadcast television signals (such as NTSC). In a standard 480i signal, the display draws 525 total lines (roughly 480 visible) divided into two alternating interlaced fields of 240 lines each.

To achieve 240p, legacy video game consoles deliberately omit the half-line timing shift that triggers interlacing. Consequently, the electron gun renders all ~240 visible lines in the exact same vertical positions every single frame at roughly 60 Hz, operating at a horizontal scanning frequency of approximately 15.7 kHz.

Scanline Structure on a 15 kHz Monitor

Standard-definition CRT televisions, broadcast video monitors (PVMs/BVMs), and traditional arcade monitors are native 15 kHz displays. When fed a native 240p signal, their hardware works in full synchronization with the source.

  • Native Beam Timing: The monitor sweeps horizontally around 15,734 times per second. Because the fields do not alternate their vertical positions, the electron beam draws across the exact same scanline coordinates on the phosphor screen during every refresh cycle.
  • Natural Black Gaps: The physical beam profile (spot size) on a 15 kHz display is vertically narrower than the distance between successive raster paths. Because the missing interlaced field is never drawn, the space where those odd or even lines would normally sit remains unilluminated.
  • The Resulting Aesthetic: These unlit gaps create distinct, authentic "scanlines"—alternating bands of brightly lit phosphors and dark, inactive lines. The thickness and sharpness of the blank spaces depend directly on the tube's television lines (TVL) of resolution, beam focus, and screen size.

Scanline Structure on a 31 kHz VGA Monitor

VGA monitors, designed primarily for personal computers, operate with a minimum horizontal scan rate of 31.5 kHz. This fundamental frequency difference completely changes how a 240p image must be handled.

  • Incompatible Native Signals: A standard 31 kHz VGA monitor cannot physically sync to a native 15 kHz 240p signal. Feeding raw 240p into a 31 kHz monitor will result in an "out of range" or "no signal" error.
  • Required Line-Doubling (480p): To display 240p content on a 31 kHz monitor, the signal must be line-doubled to 480p, doubling the horizontal sweep rate to ~31.5 kHz.
  • Elimination of Natural Scanlines: During standard line doubling, every individual 240p line is rendered twice in succession (drawing 480 active lines per frame). Because the electron beam now draws twice as many passes over the vertical plane, the dark, inactive spaces between lines disappear. The image displays as a uniform, solid grid of pixels with no natural scanline gaps.
  • Simulated Scanlines: To recreate scanlines on a 31 kHz monitor, an external device (such as a scanline generator or scaler) must artificially blank or dim every other line of the 480p output. Because VGA monitors typically feature much higher phosphor dot pitches and tighter electron beam focus than 15 kHz consumer TVs, these simulated scanlines appear significantly sharper, thinner, and more rigid than their organic 15 kHz counterparts.
