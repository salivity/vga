Why Green Gets 6 Bits in 16-Bit RGB565 Color

In 16-bit computer graphics modes, commonly known as High Color or RGB565, display systems allocate 5 bits to red, 6 bits to green, and 5 bits to blue. This design choice stems from a blend of hardware constraints and human biology: because 16 bits cannot be divided equally among three color channels, hardware designers had an extra bit to assign, and they gave it to green because the human eye is far more sensitive to green wavelengths and perceived brightness than to red or blue.

The Mathematical Constraint of 16-Bit Color

A 16-bit color system provides exactly 16 bits of memory to define the color of each individual pixel. In an additive color model using Red, Green, and Blue (RGB), dividing 16 bits across three channels leaves a mathematical remainder:

  • 16 bits divided by 3 channels = 5 bits per channel, with 1 bit remaining (\(5 \times 3 = 15\)).

Hardware designers had two main options for handling the leftover bit. One approach, known as RGB555, assigned 5 bits to each color channel and left the 16th bit unused or reserved for transparency (an alpha or overlay bit). The more efficient alternative, RGB565, utilized the full 16-bit bandwidth by granting the remaining bit to one of the primary colors, expanding that channel from 5 bits (32 intensity levels) to 6 bits (64 intensity levels).

Human Visual Biology and Luminance Sensitivity

The decision to allocate the 16th bit specifically to green is grounded in the physiology of human vision. Human retinas rely on photopic vision in well-lit conditions, mediated by cone photoreceptors categorized broadly by their peak spectral sensitivities:

  • S-cones: Sensitive to short wavelengths (blue light).
  • M-cones: Sensitive to medium wavelengths (green light).
  • L-cones: Sensitive to long wavelengths (red/yellow light).

The human eye contains vastly more M and L cones than S cones, and their spectral sensitivities heavily overlap in the green-to-yellow portion of the visible spectrum. The standard luminous efficiency function (which models human eye sensitivity) peaks at roughly 555 nanometers, right in the bright green spectrum.

Because green light contributes the vast majority of perceived luminance (brightness), standard video and imaging equations weight it far higher than red or blue. In standard luminance encoding, green accounts for approximately 59% of perceived brightness, red accounts for roughly 30%, and blue accounts for only about 11%.

Preventing Perceptual Banding

When digital color depth is low, transitions between subtle shades can produce visible "banding" or posterization artifacts. Because human vision is acutely sensitive to minute shifts in green luminance, banding in the green channel is far more noticeable and disruptive to the viewer than identical step-sizes in red or blue.

By assigning 6 bits to the green channel:

  • Red: 5 bits (\(2^5 = 32\) discrete shades)
  • Green: 6 bits (\(2^6 = 64\) discrete shades)
  • Blue: 5 bits (\(2^5 = 32\) discrete shades)

Doubling the available levels for green effectively halves the step-size between adjacent green shades, smoothing gradients where the human eye is most likely to detect flaws. The resulting RGB565 format provided the most visually appealing and artifact-free image quality possible within the strict 16-bit-per-pixel memory budget of early VGA and High Color graphics adapters.
