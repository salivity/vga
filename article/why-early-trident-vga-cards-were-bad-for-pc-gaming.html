Why Early Trident VGA Cards Were Bad for PC Gaming

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, Trident Microsystems dominated the budget PC market by offering some of the most affordable VGA and Super VGA (SVGA) cards available, such as the TVGA 8800 and 8900 series. While these cards succeeded in bringing high-resolution color graphics to the masses for general office tasks, gamers quickly found them notoriously inadequate. Trident's agonizingly slow gaming performance was the direct result of severe memory bandwidth limitations, high-latency bus architectures, and cost-cutting designs prioritized for OEM desktop builders rather than real-time rendering.

Built for Cost, Not Speed

Trident’s primary business model was delivering the lowest-cost video card possible to original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) like Packard Bell and generic PC clone builders. To hit these bottom-dollar price points, Trident designed minimalist chipsets that cut corners on internal caching, memory controllers, and component quality.

While competitors like Tseng Labs invested in high-throughput controller designs, Trident utilized cheap, slow dynamic RAM (DRAM) with high access times. The cards were designed to generate a static picture for spreadsheets and word processing in Windows 3.x rather than rapidly updating moving pixels.

The Memory Bottleneck and Heavy Wait States

Real-time gaming in the DOS era—seen in fast-paced action titles like Commander Keen, Wing Commander, and eventually Doom—depended heavily on the CPU rapidly writing large amounts of graphical data directly to video memory (VRAM).

Trident chips handled these CPU-to-VRAM writes poorly. Whenever the processor attempted to update the Trident framebuffer, the card introduced significant wait states. These wait states effectively paused the host CPU until the slow video memory could accept the data. Even if a user paired a Trident card with a powerful 386 or 486 processor, the graphics card choked the system, dragging frame rates down to a crawl.

The ISA Bus Bottleneck

Most early Trident cards ran on the aging 16-bit Industry Standard Architecture (ISA) bus, which was clocked at a sluggish 8 MHz. While other graphics manufacturers engineered aggressive memory interleaving and write buffers to maximize the limited bandwidth of the ISA bus, Trident cards operated near the absolute bottom of the bus's theoretical throughput.

When modern SVGA resolutions (such as 640x480 or 800x600 in 256 colors) were engaged, the bandwidth demands skyrocketed. Early Trident architectures simply collapsed under this load, rendering higher-color, higher-resolution gaming practically impossible.

Absence of Acceleration

Early Trident cards functioned strictly as "dumb" framebuffers. They possessed no hardware acceleration, no blitters for fast 2D sprite manipulation, and no internal logic to assist with moving data across display planes. Everything had to be calculated by the central processor and forced across the constrained memory bus. Because competitors like the Tseng Labs ET4000 or the S3 911/801 series were far more efficient at moving raw framebuffer data, Trident gained a reputation as the worst possible choice for serious gamers.

The Legacy of the "Trident Slide Show"

Trident achieved massive commercial success by making PC color graphics accessible to millions of budget-conscious buyers. However, that affordability came at the cost of rendering throughput. Because they populated millions of pre-built family PCs, an entire generation of PC gamers discovered the hard way that a budget Trident card could turn high-octane 3D games into an unplayable slide show.
