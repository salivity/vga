Why a Bent VGA Ground Pin Causes Rolling Bars

A bent ground pin on a Video Graphics Array (VGA) cable disrupts the electrical reference between the video source and the display, commonly creating faint horizontal lines known as rolling bars, scrolling lines, or video hum. This visual artifact occurs because the disconnected pin forces the analog video signal to find an alternate, high-impedance ground path, allowing alternating current (AC) power noise and ground loop interference to distort the display output.

The Role of Analog Ground in VGA

Unlike modern digital interfaces such as HDMI or DisplayPort, VGA is purely analog. It transmits visual information via voltage levels—typically ranging from 0 to 0.7 volts—across separate lines for red, green, and blue (RGB). To accurately interpret these minute voltage variations, the display needs a clean, stable zero-volt reference point.

A standard 15-pin VGA connector dedicates multiple pins strictly to grounding:

  • Pin 6: Red Ground
  • Pin 7: Green Ground
  • Pin 8: Blue Ground
  • Pin 5 & 10: Digital/Sync Grounds and Cable Detect

Floating Ground and Stray Current

When one of these ground pins is bent and fails to make contact with the female socket, that specific color or sync circuit loses its direct return path. The circuit becomes "floating," lacking a direct, low-resistance connection back to the source's ground plane.

Consequently, the return current is forced to travel through alternative paths, such as the outer cable shield, adjacent pins, or the building’s AC power wiring via the chassis grounds of the PC and monitor. These alternate paths have higher resistance and are susceptible to electromagnetic interference (EMI) from power supplies and nearby electronics.

Why Ground Loops Create "Hum Bars"

When the monitor and host computer are plugged into wall outlets, minor variations in electrical potential exist between their respective chassis. With a compromised VGA ground pin, this potential difference drives stray current through the remaining connection paths, creating a ground loop.

This stray current carries residual noise from the 50 Hz or 60 Hz electrical mains. Because the video signal is an analog voltage, this AC ripple voltage directly adds to or subtracts from the RGB signal voltages. The display interprets this unwanted voltage modulation as changes in brightness and color across horizontal scanning lines.

Why the Bars Roll

The interference appears to roll rather than remain stationary due to a slight frequency mismatch:

  1. The power grid operates at a nominal frequency, typically 60 Hz (in North America) or 50 Hz (in Europe).
  2. The monitor's vertical refresh rate is set to a similar frequency, such as 60 Hz, but internal timing crystals usually produce minor variations (e.g., 59.94 Hz or 60.05 Hz).

Because the display's refresh cycle and the power line's cycle are not perfectly synchronized, the phase of the AC hum shifts slightly with every frame drawn on the screen. This phase drift causes the horizontal bands of distorted brightness to slowly travel vertically up or down the display, creating the characteristic "rolling hum bar" effect. Straightening or replacing the damaged pin restores the direct, low-impedance ground connection, eliminating the stray voltage and stabilizing the image.
