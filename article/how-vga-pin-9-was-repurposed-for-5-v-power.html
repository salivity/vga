How VGA Pin 9 Was Repurposed for 5V Power

Originally designed as a physical key to prevent improper cable insertion, Pin 9 on the standard DE-15 Video Graphics Array (VGA) connector was later repurposed to supply +5V DC power. This article examines the historical transition of Pin 9 from an omitted mechanical guide to an active power source, detailing how the Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) used this modification to implement automated display detection and configuration via the Display Data Channel (DDC).

The Original Role of Pin 9

When IBM introduced the VGA standard in 1987, the DE-15 connector utilized a 15-pin layout across three rows. In the original specification, Pin 9 was designated as a "key" pin. To prevent incompatible cables from being used or to assist with orientation, the physical male pin on the cable connector was frequently removed, and the corresponding female hole on the graphics adapter or monitor was plugged.

During this era, graphics cards relied on simple analog sense pins (Pins 4, 11, and 12 grounded in varying combinations) to determine if a monochrome or color monitor was attached, offering no capability for bidirectional communication.

The Rise of Plug and Play and VESA DDC

By the mid-1990s, the computing industry moved toward automated hardware configuration (Plug and Play). The Video Electronics Standards Association (VESA) developed the Display Data Channel (DDC) standard to allow a host computer to query a monitor for its manufacturer information, supported resolutions, and refresh rates using Extended Display Identification Data (EDID).

DDC utilized the I²C serial bus protocol, which required dedicated lines for serial data (SDA, on Pin 12) and serial clock (SCL, on Pin 15). To store the EDID information, monitors were equipped with a small, non-volatile EEPROM chip.

Repurposing Pin 9 for +5V DC

For DDC to function reliably, the host graphics card needed to be able to read the monitor’s EEPROM even if the monitor was turned off, in a low-power standby state, or unplugged from the wall.

Under the updated VESA DDC2B specification, Pin 9 was reassigned from a mechanical key to a +5V DC power delivery line supplied directly by the host graphics adapter. This design delivered key operational benefits:

  • Host-Powered EDID Reading: The +5V supplied across Pin 9 powers the monitor's internal EEPROM and associated I²C circuitry independently of the monitor’s primary power supply. A computer can identify the monitor and set the appropriate display mode during the boot sequence, before the display has actively powered on.
  • Hot-Plugging Detection: The voltage provided via Pin 9 allows the host and display to verify a live connection, aiding in modern multi-monitor detection routines.

Electrical and Compatibility Considerations

Because earlier VGA receptacles often had Pin 9 mechanically plugged, hardware manufacturers had to phase out blocked female ports. Newer cables were manufactured with all 15 pins intact.

To ensure safety and compatibility:

  • Current Limiting: The VESA standard specified that the host must supply +5V DC at a minimum of 50mA, typically with a maximum of around 1A. Graphics cards incorporated current-limiting resistors, diodes, or self-resetting polyfuses (PTCs) on the Pin 9 trace. This prevented damage in the event that an older cable or incompatible display grounded Pin 9.
  • Isolation: The monitor's EDID EEPROM circuit uses steering diodes to isolate the Pin 9 power feed from the monitor’s internal low-voltage power supply, ensuring that the two power sources do not backfeed into each other when the monitor is switched on.
