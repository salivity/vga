Why VGA Requires Skew Compensation on Twisted Pair

Transmitting analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) signals over Category-rated twisted-pair cabling (such as Cat5e or Cat6) frequently results in visible image distortion known as color fringing. This issue occurs because the internal physical wire pairs have varying twist rates, causing them to differ in physical length. Skew compensation is the essential process of electronically realigning these misaligned color signals at the receiving end, ensuring the final image appears sharp, clear, and properly converged.

The Physics Behind Cable Skew

VGA is an analog component video format that divides its visual data into three primary color channels: Red, Green, and Blue (RGB), alongside horizontal and vertical synchronization signals. When transmitted over unshielded twisted-pair (UTP) cabling via baluns or active extenders, each color channel is mapped to a dedicated twisted pair within the cable jacket.

Standard network cables are engineered with different twist rates (twists per meter) for each pair to minimize electromagnetic interference and cross-talk between adjacent conductors. A tighter twist requires more copper wire over a given distance. Consequently, even though a network cable might measure exactly 50 meters from end to end, the internal copper conductors for one pair may be several meters longer than another.

Propagation Delay and Visual Artifacts

Electrical signals travel through copper at a fixed propagation velocity (typically about 60% to 70% the speed of light). Because the physical lengths of the pairs are unequal, the electrical signals traverse different distances. This creates a propagation delay mismatch between the Red, Green, and Blue channels.

Because VGA displays scan and render pixels in real time across the screen, a delay of merely a few nanoseconds corresponds to several pixels of horizontal displacement. Without correction, this latency differential produces distinct visual defects:

  • Color Separation: Colors separate from one another, creating noticeable red, green, or blue shadows next to sharp edges.
  • Text Blur: High-contrast elements, particularly black text on a white background, appear out of focus or develop colored fringes.
  • Loss of Detail: Fine lines and graphical details lose clarity as the superimposed color components fail to hit the screen simultaneously.

As cable runs grow longer—typically exceeding 15 to 30 meters—the cumulative delay increases significantly, rendering the displayed image unusable for detailed work.

How Skew Compensation Resolves the Issue

Skew compensation eliminates this latency mismatch by equalizing the arrival times of all transmitted channels. This can be accomplished through either active delay circuitry or physical adjustment:

  • Active (Electronic) Skew Compensation: Most modern VGA receivers or extenders feature built-in digital or analog delay lines. The receiver detects or allows manual configuration of precise delays (measured in fractions of a nanosecond) applied to the faster channels. By delaying the signals that arrive early to match the slowest signal, all color components are synchronized before being converted back into standard VGA output.
  • Passive (Physical) Delay Lines: In older or manual setups, physical delay modules with extra lengths of conductor are inserted into the faster color lines to manually balance the total physical travel distance.

Applying skew compensation is mandatory for any long-distance VGA-over-twisted-pair installation to counter the mechanical design of network cables and restore native visual alignment.
