How RAMDAC Speeds Limited Early VGA Refresh Rates

Early Video Graphics Array (VGA) adapters were fundamentally constrained by the speed of their Random Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converter (RAMDAC). Because cathode-ray tube (CRT) displays required continuous analog signals to draw images line by line, the RAMDAC had to translate digital pixel data into analog voltages in real time. The operational frequency of early RAMDACs capped the maximum "pixel clock" the system could support, directly restricting both maximum screen resolutions and the refresh rates required to eliminate visible screen flicker.

A CRT monitor relies on an electron beam scanning horizontally across the display surface from top to bottom. To maintain an image, the entire frame must be rewritten repeatedly at a specific frequency, known as the vertical refresh rate. The total number of pixels processed per second—the pixel clock—is calculated by multiplying the horizontal pixel count (including horizontal blanking intervals) by the vertical line count (including vertical blanking intervals) and multiplying that product by the target refresh rate.

When IBM introduced the VGA standard in 1987, the default configuration relied on discrete RAMDACs, such as the Inmos IMSG171, which operated at a maximum frequency of roughly 28 MHz. This clock speed was sufficient for standard VGA output: 640x480 resolution at 60 Hz required a pixel clock of approximately 25.175 MHz, while 720x400 text mode at 70 Hz required 28.322 MHz. However, this left almost no headroom for higher configurations.

Raising the refresh rate to reduce eye strain, or increasing the resolution to 800x600, instantly broke these limitations. For example, rendering 800x600 at a standard 60 Hz demands a pixel clock of roughly 40 MHz due to the overhead of blanking intervals. Attempting an ergonomic 75 Hz or 85 Hz refresh rate at 640x480 requires clock speeds exceeding 31 MHz and 36 MHz respectively. Because early RAMDACs physically could not sample memory, convert digital palette values to analog red, green, and blue voltages, and transmit them fast enough, pushing past their rated megahertz thresholds resulted in corrupted signals, out-of-sync displays, or severe overheating.

To circumvent these hardware limits without faster RAMDACs, manufacturers often resorted to interlacing, such as in the 8514/A standard, which refreshed alternating lines of the screen each pass to cut bandwidth demands in half at the cost of noticeable flicker. It was not until the early to mid-1990s—when standalone RAMDAC speeds jumped to 80 MHz, 135 MHz, and beyond, eventually being integrated directly into the graphics processing die—that graphics cards could deliver the high pixel clocks necessary for flicker-free refresh rates at resolutions beyond standard VGA.
