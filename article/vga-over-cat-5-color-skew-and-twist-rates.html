VGA Over Cat5 Color Skew and Twist Rates

Transmitting analog Video Graphics Array (VGA) signals over standard Category 5 (Cat5) twisted-pair cable often produces visible color separation, an artifact commonly referred to as "skew." This problem occurs because each twisted pair inside a Cat5 cable is engineered with a different twist rate to prevent internal crosstalk. This article explains the relationship between twist pitch, physical conductor length, propagation delay, and how these factors misalign the red, green, and blue color channels on a display.

The Engineering Purpose of Differing Twist Rates

Cat5 cable contains four balanced twisted pairs. If two adjacent pairs share the exact same twist pitch (the number of twists per inch or meter), their electrical conductors remain parallel over long distances. This causes capacitive and inductive coupling, known as crosstalk, which corrupts the data being transmitted. To prevent this interference, manufacturers intentionally wind each of the four pairs at a slightly different twist rate.

How Twist Pitch Changes Physical Conductor Length

Because each pair is twisted at a unique rate, the physical length of the copper wire within each pair is not equal, even though the outer jacket of the cable measures the same length. A pair with a tight twist rate requires significantly more physical copper wire to traverse a 100-foot run of cable than a pair with a loose twist rate. Consequently, while the cable jacket may measure 100 feet from end to end, the internal copper conductors can differ in physical length by several feet.

Propagation Delay Across Color Channels

In a standard VGA-over-Cat5 passive or active transmission setup, the Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) analog video signals are mapped to separate twisted pairs:

  • Pair 1 might carry the Red signal.
  • Pair 2 might carry the Green signal.
  • Pair 3 might carry the Blue signal.

Electrical signals travel through copper at a specific nominal velocity of propagation (NVP), typically around 65% to 70% of the speed of light. Because the NVP is roughly identical for all conductors, a signal traveling on a longer conductor takes more time to reach the destination than a signal traveling on a shorter conductor.

For every foot of additional wire length caused by a tighter twist pitch, the signal is delayed by roughly 1.5 nanoseconds. Over typical transmission distances (100 to 300 feet), this propagation differential—or pair-to-pair skew—can easily reach 20 to 50 nanoseconds between the fastest and slowest pairs.

Manifestation as Color Separation on the Display

VGA is an analog raster format. An electron gun or digital timing controller paints the display line by line, moving rapidly from left to right. Because the display scans horizontally at high frequencies, time directly translates to horizontal position on the screen:

  1. Simultaneous Transmission: The video source sends the red, green, and blue components of a pixel at the exact same moment.
  2. Delayed Arrival: The loosely twisted pair delivers its color signal to the monitor first. The tightly twisted pair delivers its color signal a few nanoseconds later.
  3. Spatial Misalignment: By the time the delayed color signal arrives, the monitor’s scanning beam has already moved further along the horizontal line.

This delay causes the delayed color channel to be drawn several pixels to the right of the earlier arriving channels. High-contrast edges, such as black text on a white background, clearly reveal this separation: one side of the text displays a noticeable red outline, while the opposite side displays a cyan, green, or blue shadow.

Solutions to Skew Delay

Color separation can be eliminated either by using "low-skew" Cat5 cable—where all pairs are twisted at identical or nearly identical rates at the expense of higher crosstalk—or by installing an active receiver with skew-compensation capabilities. Skew compensators add variable electronic delay lines to the faster-arriving color channels, aligning all three signals in time before they are rendered on the screen.
