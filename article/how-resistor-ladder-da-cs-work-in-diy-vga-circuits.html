How Resistor Ladder DACs Work in DIY VGA Circuits

This article explains how resistor ladder digital-to-analog converters (DACs) convert digital microcontroller pins into analog RGB signals for DIY Video Graphics Array (VGA) displays. You will learn the core operational principles of resistor ladders, how VGA electrical standards dictate component values, and the exact methods used to allocate digital pins to generate 8-bit (RGB332) and 16-bit (RGB565) color palettes.

What is a Resistor Ladder DAC?

A resistor ladder DAC is a passive electrical circuit that converts discrete digital binary values into an analog voltage output using a network of precision resistors. In digital circuits, outputs are binary states—typically high (3.3V or 5V) or low (0V). Because cathode-ray tube (CRT) and modern analog VGA displays require a continuously variable voltage to determine the brightness of an electron beam or pixel sub-element, a DAC is required to bridge the digital and analog domains.

Two primary topologies exist for resistor ladder DACs:

  1. Binary-Weighted Ladder: Uses resistors whose values double with each increasing bit of significance (\(R\), \(2R\), \(4R\), \(8R\), etc.). The most significant bit (MSB) connects through the smallest resistance to allow maximum current, while the least significant bit (LSB) connects through the largest resistance.
  2. R-2R Ladder: Uses only two resistor values (\(R\) and \(2R\)) arranged in a repeating ladder structure. This eliminates the need for numerous odd-value precision resistors and provides consistent output impedance.

In DIY VGA circuits, both configurations are common. Binary-weighted ladders are often favored for low bit depths (2 to 5 bits per channel) due to their low component count, while R-2R ladders are preferred when higher bit depths demand matched temperature coefficients and tighter tolerances.

VGA Signal Standards and Electrical Constraints

To properly design a resistor ladder for VGA, the circuit must adhere strictly to the VGA electrical specification:

  • Voltage Range: The color lines (Red, Green, Blue) expect an analog voltage between \(0.0\text{V}\) (black/off) and \(0.7\text{V}\) peak-to-peak (maximum saturation). Exceeding \(0.7\text{V}\) can cause image clipping or damage the monitor's input stages.
  • Termination Impedance: Inside the monitor, each color channel is terminated to ground with a \(75\,\Omega\) resistor.
  • Source Impedance: The video cable and driving circuit are designed for a characteristic impedance of \(75\,\Omega\) to prevent signal reflections and ghosting artifacts.

Because the monitor's internal \(75\,\Omega\) resistor forms the bottom half of a voltage divider network with the DAC, all resistor calculations must account for this load.

Calculating Resistor Values for the DAC

When all digital output pins for a single color channel are driven high, the parallel combination of the DAC resistors alongside the monitor's \(75\,\Omega\) termination must produce exactly \(0.7\text{V}\) at the input of the display.

Given a microcontroller output voltage \(V_{OH}\) (typically \(3.3\text{V}\) or \(5.0\text{V}\)), the required equivalent source resistance \(R_{eq}\) of the ladder can be calculated using the standard voltage divider equation:

\[V_{out} = V_{OH} \times \frac{75\,\Omega}{R_{eq} + 75\,\Omega}\]

Rearranging to solve for \(R_{eq}\) when targeting \(V_{out} = 0.7\text{V}\):

\[R_{eq} = 75 \times \left(\frac{V_{OH} - 0.7}{0.7}\right)\]

For a \(3.3\text{V}\) microcontroller (such as an ESP32 or Raspberry Pi Pico): \[R_{eq} = 75 \times \left(\frac{3.3 - 0.7}{0.7}\right) \approx 278.57\,\Omega\]

For a \(5.0\text{V}\) microcontroller (such as an ATmega328P): \[R_{eq} = 75 \times \left(\frac{5.0 - 0.7}{0.7}\right) \approx 460.71\,\Omega\]

The individual resistors assigned to each bit are then chosen such that their parallel resistance equals \(R_{eq}\), with conductances scaled in powers of two.

Generating 8-Bit Color (RGB332)

An 8-bit color setup assigns one full byte to represent a single pixel, yielding \(2^8 = 256\) possible colors. Because human vision is unevenly sensitive to different light wavelengths—most sensitive to green, moderately to red, and least to blue—the 8 bits are partitioned using the RGB332 standard:

  • Red: 3 bits (\(2^3 = 8\) voltage levels, from \(0.0\text{V}\) to \(0.7\text{V}\))
  • Green: 3 bits (\(2^3 = 8\) voltage levels, from \(0.0\text{V}\) to \(0.7\text{V}\))
  • Blue: 2 bits (\(2^2 = 4\) voltage levels, from \(0.0\text{V}\) to \(0.7\text{V}\))

Implementation:

For a 3-bit channel (Red or Green), three GPIO pins connect to the color line through three binary-weighted resistors (\(R\), \(2R\), \(4R\)). For a 3.3V system targeting an equivalent resistance of \(\approx 278\,\Omega\):

  • Bit 2 (MSB): \(\approx 487\,\Omega\) (Standard E96 value: \(487\,\Omega\) or parallel pairs)
  • Bit 1: \(\approx 974\,\Omega\) (Standard value: \(1\,\text{k}\Omega\))
  • Bit 0 (LSB): \(\approx 1948\,\Omega\) (Standard value: \(2\,\text{k}\Omega\))

For the 2-bit Blue channel, two pins connect through values calculated to provide equivalent \(0.7\text{V}\) scaling across four discrete states (\(00\), \(01\), \(10\), \(11\)).

Generating 16-Bit Color (RGB565)

A 16-bit color setup uses two bytes per pixel, yielding \(2^{16} = 65,536\) colors. This format is universally known as RGB565:

  • Red: 5 bits (\(2^5 = 32\) voltage levels)
  • Green: 6 bits (\(2^6 = 64\) voltage levels)
  • Blue: 5 bits (\(2^5 = 32\) voltage levels)

Green receives the extra bit due to the human eye's peak spectral sensitivity around \(555\,\text{nm}\).

Implementation:

Implementing RGB565 via discrete binary-weighted resistors requires 16 high-speed digital output pins on the microcontroller and 16 individual resistors:

  • Red Channel (5 bits): Five resistors scaled as \(R, 2R, 4R, 8R, 16R\).
  • Green Channel (6 bits): Six resistors scaled as \(R, 2R, 4R, 8R, 16R, 32R\).
  • Blue Channel (5 bits): Five resistors scaled identically to the Red channel.

At 5 and 6 bits of resolution, resistor tolerances become critical. Standard \(5\%\) tolerance resistors introduce non-linearities and monotonic errors (where an increment in digital value results in a lower analog voltage). Designs utilizing 16-bit color should use metal-film resistors with \(1\%\) or \(0.1\%\) tolerance to preserve color fidelity and prevent visual banding across gradients.
