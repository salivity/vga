Why VGA 70 Hz Affects CRT Vertical Frame Sizing

When switching a Video Graphics Array (VGA) display mode to a 70 Hz vertical refresh rate, older Cathode Ray Tube (CRT) monitors often exhibit a noticeable change in vertical frame size—frequently shrinking the image or introducing black bars. This behavior occurs because early CRT monitors relied on purely analog deflection circuits governed by continuous electrical waveforms rather than digital scaling. When the vertical refresh rate changes, the duration of the scan cycle shifts, directly altering the amplitude of the magnetic deflection driving the electron beam.

The Mechanism of Vertical Deflection

A CRT forms an image by firing an electron beam across a phosphor-coated glass surface. Magnetic coils, known as the deflection yoke, pull this beam horizontally and vertically across the screen.

To draw a complete frame from top to bottom, the vertical deflection circuit generates a linear sawtooth current. As the current ramps up smoothly, the magnetic field intensifies, drawing the beam from the top of the tube down to the bottom. Once the beam reaches the bottom, the current rapidly collapses during the "vertical blanking interval," snapping the beam back to the top to begin the next cycle.

The Impact of Frequency on Sawtooth Amplitude

In purely analog or early multi-sync monitors, the vertical deflection circuit often uses an analog integrator with a fixed ramp rate (slew rate). The physical height of the image directly corresponds to the peak-to-peak amplitude of the sawtooth current:

  • At 60 Hz: The time period of a single frame is approximately 16.67 milliseconds. The integrator circuit has 16.67 ms to ramp up its current, allowing the deflection field to reach maximum intended strength and push the beam fully from top to bottom.
  • At 70 Hz: The frame duration drops to approximately 14.29 milliseconds. Because the ramp duration is cut short by roughly 2.4 milliseconds, the analog integrator has less time to charge.

Without an automatic gain compensation circuit to increase the charging slope, the peak current reached before the flyback trigger is lower. A lower peak magnetic deflection translates directly to less vertical travel of the electron beam, causing the frame to appear vertically compressed (underscanned).

VGA Timing Standards and Scanline Counts

The original 1987 IBM VGA standard compounded this issue by using different vertical scanline totals across common modes:

  1. 640x480 Graphics Mode (60 Hz): Runs with a vertical timing of 525 total lines (480 active display lines plus blanking lines) at a horizontal scan rate of 31.468 kHz.
  2. 720x400 Text Mode (70 Hz): Runs with a vertical timing of 449 total lines (400 active display lines plus blanking lines) at the exact same 31.468 kHz horizontal scan rate.

Because the horizontal sweep frequency remained identical (~31.5 kHz) between these modes, early monitors did not need complex multi-frequency horizontal circuitry to display both. However, because the total line count fell from 525 to 449 lines to accommodate the faster 70 Hz refresh rate within the same pixel clock, the vertical beam traversed fewer total horizontal passes per frame.

Lack of Per-Mode Digital Memory

Modern LCD panels and late-era CRTs feature digital microcontrollers with non-volatile memory (EEPROM) that store dedicated geometry profiles (height, width, pincushion, and position) for dozens of standard display timings. When an input switches to 70 Hz, a digitally controlled monitor detects the sync signal polarity and frequency, instantly loading the calibrated size settings for that specific mode.

Older CRTs lacked digital processors. They relied on global analog potentiometers—either external adjustment knobs or internal trimpots on the circuit board—to set vertical size (V-SIZE) and vertical linearity. Adjusting the vertical size knob to make a 70 Hz DOS text screen fill the glass meant that switching to a 60 Hz graphical application would stretch the frame beyond the visible boundaries (overscan), cutting off the top and bottom of the picture.
