How CRT to LCD Changed Classic VGA Games

The transition from cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors to modern flat-panel LCD displays fundamentally altered the visual presentation of classic Video Graphics Array (VGA) games. During the late 1980s and 1990s, game artists crafted graphics specifically tailored to the optical properties and limitations of CRT technology. When flat panels replaced CRTs, their fixed-pixel matrices, razor-sharp edges, and uniform lighting stripped away the natural blending, color depth, and intended aspect ratios that defined the original VGA aesthetic.

The Loss of Natural Hardware Blending

Classic VGA games—typically rendered in Mode 13h at a 320x200 resolution—relied heavily on the physics of CRT monitors to display soft, cohesive images. In a CRT, an electron gun scans lines across a curved glass screen, exciting colored phosphors that emit light with a slight halo effect, often called "beam bloom."

Because phosphors bleed into adjacent areas, hard pixel edges naturally softened. Artists exploited this phenomenon using dithering—placing alternating colors in checkerboard patterns to simulate gradients, shading, and perceived colors well beyond the hardware's 256-color limit. On a CRT, these patterns blended into smooth transitions and simulated transparency. On modern flat panels, where every pixel is an isolated, rigid square, that hardware blending disappears, leaving dithering patterns exposed as harsh, distracting checkerboards.

Fixed Pixel Grids and Uneven Scaling

CRTs were multisync analog devices with no native resolution. A CRT could adjust its electron beam to draw a 320x200 or 640x480 image across the entire screen surface without interpolation artifacts.

Flat panels, conversely, possess a fixed physical grid of liquid crystals. Displaying a low-resolution VGA game on a 1080p or 4K panel requires upscaling. Standard nearest-neighbor scaling makes the image excessively sharp and blocky—a look often mistakenly labeled "authentic retro"—while standard bilinear scaling produces an unnatural, muddy blur. Furthermore, non-integer scaling factors cause uneven pixel widths, leading to noticeable shimmering during horizontal or vertical scrolling.

Non-Square Pixels and Aspect Ratio Distortion

VGA graphics were built around a mathematical quirk: 320x200 is a 16:10 resolution, but CRT computer monitors were built with a physical 4:3 aspect ratio. The monitor physically stretched the image vertically to fill the screen, resulting in non-square pixels with an aspect ratio of 1:1.2 (taller than they were wide).

Game artists drew assets to account for this stretch. Circles, faces, and UI elements drawn in graphics software looked compressed on modern 1:1 square-pixel displays unless corrected. When early flat panels displayed these games with modern pixel mapping, characters appeared squat and circular crosshairs or shields became horizontal ovals.

Contrast, Motion Blur, and Black Levels

CRTs delivered instantaneous response times and deep black levels. Because phosphors light up and decay almost instantly, CRTs maintained exceptional motion clarity during fast scrolling with zero ghosting. Furthermore, areas without active beam strikes produced true blacks.

Early LCD panels introduced significant motion blur and gray, backlit blacks that degraded high-contrast DOS titles like DOOM or Commander Keen. While modern OLED and high-refresh panels have resolved contrast and response limitations, the absence of natural scanlines, phosphor bloom, and CRT geometry means that raw VGA graphics remain permanently transformed unless restored via sophisticated CRT emulation shaders.
