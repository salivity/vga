Why 31 kHz VGA Displays Show Scanlines on 240p Games

When line-doubled 240p video signals are sent to a native 31 kHz Video Graphics Array (VGA) cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitor, visible empty black lines often appear between the rendered image rows. This article explains why these gaps—commonly referred to as scanlines—manifest on high-frequency displays, detailing the physical mechanics of the monitor's electron beam spot size, the spacing of the 480p raster grid, and the role of line-doubling hardware configurations.

Electron Beam Spot Size and Focus

The primary physical reason black lines appear on a native 31 kHz CRT monitor is the narrow diameter of its electron beam. PC VGA monitors were engineered to resolve high resolutions such as 800x600, 1024x768, or higher. To cleanly display these dense resolutions without blurring adjacent pixels, manufacturers calibrated the electron guns with an extremely fine focus and small spot size.

When a 240p game is line-doubled to 480p, it matches the native 31.5 kHz horizontal scan rate of standard VGA by drawing 480 active horizontal lines. However, because the CRT's electron beam is tightly focused and physically thinner than the vertical height allocated to each line in a 480-line raster, the beam does not illuminate the entire vertical space of each scan path. The unilluminated phosphors between the passes remain completely dark, creating empty black lines.

Raster Geometry and Vertical Spacing

A native 31 kHz display operates at approximately double the horizontal frequency of a standard 15 kHz consumer television. While a 15 kHz TV draws roughly 240 lines per 60 Hz frame (leaving wide physical gaps because it skips the alternate interlaced field), a 31 kHz display draws all 480 lines sequentially within the same timeframe.

Even though twice as many lines are drawn compared to a standard 240p signal, the physical pitch and raster distribution of a high-resolution PC monitor preserve distinct gaps. On larger or higher-end VGA monitors, the dot pitch and shadow mask or aperture grille are so fine that 480 distinct lines still leave substantial unexcited phosphor area between them, preserving the appearance of gaps.

Intentional Scanline Insertion in Doublers

In addition to the physical properties of the display, the line-doubling device itself often introduces empty black lines electronically. Devices such as the Open Source Scan Converter (OSSC), RetroTINK, or classic arcade linedoublers frequently offer scanline emulation modes.

Instead of repeating each line twice (Line 1, Line 1, Line 2, Line 2), some hardware configurations output an alternating black line pattern (Line 1, Black, Line 2, Black) to simulate the authentic look of a 15 kHz broadcast monitor. If such a mode is enabled on the scaler, the black lines on the 31 kHz monitor are not merely physical beam gaps, but actual zero-luminance data actively transmitted in the video signal.
