Why Graphic Designers Chose CRT Over Early LCDs

During the late 1990s and early 2000s, professional graphic designers steadfastly avoided early liquid crystal display (LCD) monitors, choosing instead to keep heavy, bulky flat-face cathode ray tube (CRT) displays on their desks. While early LCDs offered sleek form factors and reduced power consumption, they suffered from significant technical compromises in color reproduction, black levels, viewing angles, and resolution scaling. For professionals whose livelihoods depended on absolute visual fidelity and reliable print-proofing, flat-face CRTs remained the uncontested industry standard until LCD technology matured years later.

Superior Color Accuracy and Calibration

Color fidelity is the single most critical factor in graphic design and prepress work. Flat-face CRTs produced color by firing electron beams at red, green, and blue phosphors, yielding true, continuous analog color transitions and wide color gamuts.

In contrast, early consumer and prosumer LCDs predominantly utilized Twisted Nematic (TN) panels. These panels were typically limited to 6-bit color per channel, using spatial dithering and frame rate control (FRC) to approximate 16.7 million colors (8-bit). This limitation introduced noticeable banding in gradients and crushed subtle color variations. Furthermore, calibration hardware like colorimeters struggled with the inconsistent color temperatures and non-linear gamma curves typical of early CCFL (cold-cathode fluorescent lamp) backlights.

True Black Levels and Dynamic Range

A CRT monitor generates black simply by not illuminating the phosphors in that specific area of the screen, resulting in near-perfect, inky black levels and exceptionally high contrast ratios.

Early LCDs relied on constant backlighting that was blocked by liquid crystal shutters. Because these shutters could never block 100 percent of the light, blacks consistently appeared dark gray or milky blue. This "backlight bleed" destroyed shadow detail, making it impossible for designers to accurately gauge shadow depth, contrast thresholds, and tonal separation in dark photography or illustrations.

Consistent Off-Axis Viewing Angles

Flat-face CRTs (such as those using Sony Trinitron or Mitsubishi Diamondtron aperture-grille tubes) offered near-180-degree viewing angles with virtually zero color shift or luminance decay. Whether a designer sat directly in front of the screen or leaned back to evaluate a composition, the image looked the same.

Early LCDs suffered from extreme viewing-angle distortion. Shifting just a few inches up, down, or sideways caused immediate gamma shifts, color inversions, and luminance loss. A solid color field could appear as a gradient simply because the top and bottom of the display were viewed at slightly different angles from the designer’s eye level, making precise proofing impossible.

Multi-Sync Resolution Flexibility

Graphic design often requires working across multiple screen resolutions to test layouts for different outputs. CRTs operated natively across a broad spectrum of resolutions without image degradation. Because the electron beam simply scanned lines across a continuous phosphor layer, a CRT could display 1024x768, 1280x1024, or 1600x1200 with native sharpness.

Early LCD panels were bound to a rigid pixel matrix, known as the native resolution. Displaying any resolution lower than the native grid required digital interpolation and scaling, which introduced severe blurriness, aliasing, and artifacting.

Elimination of Curvature Distortion

Traditional curved CRTs introduced geometric distortion that warped straight lines at the screen edges. The introduction of flat-face CRTs eliminated this final physical flaw through specialized glass lenses and flatter tension-mask technologies, delivering perfectly straight lines, accurate grids, and edge-to-edge uniformity. Combined with their inherent analog color strengths, flat-face CRTs gave designers the precision they needed until advanced In-Plane Switching (IPS) panels and dedicated high-gamut LCDs finally matched their performance.
